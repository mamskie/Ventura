Two Italians Killed in Kuwait Drone Attack Blamed on Iran; Trump Says War Ends 'Whenever I Want It'

Two Italian technicians were killed on Thursday in a drone attack on an air defence surveillance station in Kuwait, in an incident Italy and Kuwait blame on Iran — and Tehran firmly denies. The deaths, confirmed within hours by Rome and Kuwait City, have pulled two more European nationals into a regional war that began in February and has already spread across the Gulf.

The two men worked for Rheinmetall Italia, the Italian unit of the German defence group Rheinmetall, and were at the station when it was hit. Two more people were injured: a Pakistani national and a captain in the Kuwaiti army. As diplomats traded accusations, President Donald Trump said late on Thursday that the United States had “victory at hand” in the wider conflict with Iran, and that it would end “whenever I want it to be, to be honest.”

What happened in Kuwait

The attack struck on Thursday, November 26, hitting what Kuwait’s Defence Ministry described as a “vital facility” — an air defence surveillance station. Kuwait’s armed forces said the two Italians were killed by falling debris following Iranian attacks on the Gulf state. Italy’s foreign minister, Antonio Tajani, told the Senate in Rome that the men died after “a powerful explosion” at the site, according to information coming from Kuwait.

Tajani said the two were technicians for Rheinmetall Italia. Kuwait’s Defence Ministry later confirmed that the Italians had died of their injuries, after initially reporting that four people had been wounded by falling debris. It said the other two injured were Kuwaiti and Pakistani nationals, and that the attack had caused material damage.

Italy’s response was swift but measured. Tajani told the Italian news agency AGI that Italy would lodge a formal complaint with the Iranian embassy, while urging caution and saying there was no need to attack Arab countries bordering Iran that were not involved in the war.

The incident quickly became the most-searched news story in the United States, with “iran attacks kuwait” surging to the top of Google’s trending searches at more than 10,000 searches — a sign of how closely Americans are watching a war that, nine months in, keeps widening.

Tehran denies involvement — and offers a joint investigation

Iran has denied any role in the attack. The country’s senior armed forces spokesperson, Abolfazl Shekarchi, said no offensive or military operations by Iranian forces had taken place in the region, according to Iranian state media. Iran’s embassy in Rome categorically denied the claims of an Iranian drone attack on a military site in Kuwait in a post on X.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi went further, calling the incident “suspicious” and writing on X that it “looks like a precooked recipe for war.” He said he had spoken with his Italian counterpart, Tajani, and expressed regret over the deaths, while stressing that Tehran had no role or involvement in the incident. Iran, he said, was ready to cooperate with the governments of Kuwait and Italy in investigating what happened.

A spokesperson for Iran’s military told state media that “these accusations should be viewed as a cover for continued malevolence and an attempt to exacerbate insecurity in the region” by U.S. and Israeli forces. Araghchi said Tehran was prepared to undertake joint investigations with Kuwait “to identify the origin of the incident and parties involved in it,” according to a post on his Telegram channel.

The denial sits against a complicated recent history. Earlier in November, Iran said it had targeted U.S. drone and missile sites in Kuwait after the United States struck Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. That admission is why Kuwait and Italy find an Iranian hand in Thursday’s attack plausible — and why Tehran is insisting on a joint inquiry rather than accepting blame.

The wider war gives both readings room to grow. The regional conflict erupted on February 28, when the U.S. and Israel launched attacks on Iran, and it has since widened to include Iranian strikes on Israel and Gulf Arab states hosting U.S. military bases. It has dragged in global powers, upended the world’s energy and transport sectors, and brought chaos to areas of the region that are usually peaceful.

Why this killing matters beyond Kuwait

The deaths matter because they widen the war’s circle of victims — and of claimants. Two Italian civilians, working for a European defence contractor at a Gulf air defence site, are now dead in a conflict neither Italy nor Kuwait started. Rome’s formal complaint, even couched in caution, puts a NATO ally on record against Tehran; Kuwait’s account of an attack on a “vital facility” puts a Gulf monarchy in the same place.

The timing is equally charged. Israel said on Thursday that it was prepared to carry out strikes against Iran “at any moment,” even without U.S. forces joining the effort, according to military spokesman Effie Defrin on Israeli broadcaster Kan. The comments came after U.S. envoy Jared Kushner told a podcast on Wednesday that Israel “needs to get out of the constant wartime mindset.” Israel has been pushing the Trump administration to back another offensive against Tehran, with Reuters reporting on November 18 that Israeli spy chief Roman Gofman pushed for joint strikes when he met U.S. officials earlier in the month.

Trump, speaking late on Thursday on PBS and during a Thanksgiving call with U.S. military personnel, said the U.S. had “victory at hand” and repeated that victory would come “whenever I want it to be, to be honest.” He said Washington was still trying to give Iran “every chance to make a deal and save a lot of lives,” but added that he was not sure whether Tehran would agree. “Some people question why. Are they crazy? Maybe they are,” he said.

What happens next turns on the investigation Araghchi has offered and the evidence Kuwait produces. A joint inquiry could cool the episode; a disputed one could hand Israel’s case for another offensive its sharpest argument yet — and leave two Italian families, and a region braced for escalation, waiting for an answer that may not come quickly.
