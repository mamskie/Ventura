Brad Pitt Returns as Cliff Booth in David Fincher's Tarantino-Written Sequel

Brad Pitt is back as Cliff Booth, and the stuntman who stole Quentin Tarantino's 2019 film "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood" finally has his own movie. "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth" opened in IMAX theaters on November 25 and reaches Netflix on December 23 — and the early verdicts suggest the wait was worth it.

USA TODAY gave the film three-and-a-half stars out of four, calling it a delightful way to spend two-and-a-half hours in 1970s Los Angeles with Pitt. It is a sequel with an unusual pedigree: written by Tarantino, directed by David Fincher, and built around a character Pitt has already won an Oscar for playing.

The film picks up eight years after the original, in June 1977, and finds Cliff a long way from the stunt lots where he made his name. Hollywood has mostly moved on without him — but some of its players still need his particular skills.

What happened

Cliff has left stunt work behind. He now does odd jobs to pay the bills, working as a movie-theater projectionist and a part-time bartender, and he has an off-the-books gig as well: fixer for Hollywood types. When a filmmaker gets his Oscar stolen by a young gay lover, Cliff is the man who helps out. He is also a living urban legend in Los Angeles, where everyone wants to ask about the night he killed those hippies, or kicking Bruce Lee's butt.

The story gets moving when talent agent Karen Crosby, played by Elizabeth Debicki, calls Cliff on behalf of her client, TV star Charlie Peters, played by Scott Caan. Cliff cannot work out why Charlie wants to hire him — Charlie does all his own stunts, and has a bad reputation in that community. It takes about 90 minutes to learn why: Charlie has made a huge mistake, and a pair of Filipino gangster brothers, played by Matt Groove and JB Tadena, are causing trouble in the City of Angels.

Peter Weller plays his TV-adoring dad Gus, superbly deadpan, and Cliff is at his most loving around him. Caan, as the mercurial, insecure man-child Charlie, has never been better — it is eerie how much he resembles his legendary father, James Caan, in appearance and performance.

Then there is Debicki, six-foot-two and the movie's secret weapon. Amid the banter, violence and Tinseltown intrigue, there is an offbeat romance in which Cliff tries to woo Karen — her height reminds him of Sheena, Queen of the Jungle — and Karen is initially not having it. Yet Debicki lets the agent's walls crumble as Karen, like the audience, inevitably falls for Cliff, kinks and all.

Fincher gives the town a timeless sparkle. There is electric energy to Cliff's misadventures, like the sequence in which he goes wild driving a scrap heap at a demolition derby. The filmmaker sticks close to Tarantino's signature style, so there is no mistake: this is a loud and proud Tarantino jam, full of banter, oddball characters and odes to old-school film and television.

A Tarantino script, a Fincher film

The film exists at all because of an unusual handoff. Tarantino wrote the script, then decided against making it the tenth and final film he directs. "[Pitt] was the one I felt I was really letting down when I didn't do it," Tarantino recently admitted. "I felt bad about that." A disappointed Pitt kept the project alive by turning to Fincher, his collaborator on "Seven," "Fight Club" and "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button."

Tarantino said he was thrilled that Fincher wanted the material. "One of the things that made this all work was my excitement over David doing my material," he said. "The fact that a director of David's caliber would spend a year-and-a-half of his life doing my material was wonderful. It said a lot about what was on the page."

Fincher did not simply shoot Tarantino's script as written. He streamlined it, cutting 60 pages, and the result is a darker, more ruthless movie than its predecessor — set in a world Tarantino created, but directed in Fincher's own style. Editor Kirk Baxter has a great line for it: "It's like 'Shampoo,' but with a body count."

"The thing I realized was it's an L.A. story," Fincher said. "It's a Hal Ashby kind of world, and there's great compassion for these characters who have, in some ways, failed themselves or failed each other. I said, 'I think it's a Hal Ashby movie, with great Robert Towne women at the core of it.'"

The original film sets a high bar for its sequel. "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood" was a smashing success, grossing roughly $400 million worldwide and earning 10 Oscar nominations, including the one that resulted in Pitt's first acting Oscar. The sequel asks audiences to follow Cliff alone, eight years on, after Rick Dalton has outgrown him — in a 1977 Hollywood that has mostly left its old stuntman behind.

Why it matters

The film matters first as a test of a rare creative gamble — one of the most exacting directors alive making another auteur's movie. Directors of Fincher's standing almost never spend a year and a half on someone else's script, and Tarantino almost never lets anyone else direct his words. If the combination works — and the early reviews say it largely does — a shared cinematic language can survive a change of hands.

It also matters because of what Tarantino chose not to do. By declining to make this his tenth film, he kept his long-promised final movie unmade — and the question of what it will be wide open. Cliff Booth, meanwhile, gets to live on as a character rather than a farewell: a laid-back, scruffy hero whose story now belongs to two directors instead of one.

The release plan matters too. An IMAX opening gives the film a theatrical event window, and the Netflix debut on December 23 puts it before a vast holiday audience that never bought a cinema ticket. For a movie steeped in old Hollywood — projection booths, demolition derbies, bars where out-of-work stuntmen drink — streaming may be where most viewers finally meet Cliff Booth again.

Like so many of Tarantino's iconic characters, there is an infectious honor to Pitt's Booth, even though he is capable of some bad, rather illegal things. Yet the actor also lends him a suave, introverted quality, content with a quiet life when not being a magnet for trouble. Full of Easter eggs and cameos, it is another immersive trip into the weird corners of Hollywood — one that begs for more misadventure.
