A 5.1-Magnitude Earthquake Shook Central California — and the Shaking Reached San Francisco

A magnitude 5.1 earthquake struck Central California on Thursday night, jolting residents across the region and sending searches for “earthquake” surging across the United States. The quake hit at 10:49 p.m., with an epicenter roughly five miles east of Soledad in Monterey County, near Pinnacles National Park, according to the U.S. Geological Survey.

The shaking travelled far beyond the epicenter. Residents in San Francisco reported feeling it on social media, and in Marina, on the Monterey Bay coast, one resident received a U.S. Geological Survey ShakeAlert and then felt the ground move. There were no immediate reports of injuries or major damage, according to San Benito County emergency officials, and the National Weather Service issued no tsunami warnings.

What happened

The U.S. Geological Survey first reported the quake as a magnitude 5.4, then revised the figure down to 5.1 as more data came in — a normal part of how preliminary earthquake measurements are refined. The epicenter was located near Pinnacles National Park, which straddles the border between Monterey and San Benito counties. The KSBW newsroom in Monterey-Salinas put the center 21 kilometers northeast of Soledad, 40 kilometers east-southeast of Salinas and 33 kilometers south-southeast of Hollister, while NBC Bay Area placed it 6.2 miles northwest of Pinnacles and 20.4 miles southeast of Hollister. The USGS put the depth at 3.3 miles.

The timing made a startling night worse. The quake struck during a massive atmospheric river storm that has swept the Bay Area, dumping heavy rain and triggering flooding, power outages and downed trees. For residents already watching the weather, the shaking was a second emergency on a night of flooded roads and power cuts across the region.

For at least one resident of Marina, there was warning before the shaking. The resident told KSBW they received a USGS ShakeAlert and felt shaking, and the alert advised people to “drop, cover, hold on” if the shaking intensified. It was a small, real-world test of the warning system California has spent years building.

The 5.1 was not the area’s first jolt of the week. On Wednesday night at 9:03 p.m., a magnitude 3.3 earthquake was recorded 3.1 miles east of Pinnacles at a depth of 4.3 miles, according to the U.S. Geological Survey, and the region was also hit by a magnitude 4.1 earthquake on November 4. The Sacramento Bee, which tracks the area’s seismic activity, reported two earthquakes there in the last seven days — and 1,006 earthquakes over magnitude 1.0 in the last year, including 40 over magnitude 3.0.

A deeper look: a restless stretch of the San Andreas Fault

Thursday’s quake happened along the San Andreas Fault, according to experts cited by the San Francisco Chronicle — the fault system capable of producing major earthquakes that runs the length of California. The fault is the boundary between the Pacific and North American plates, where the Earth’s crust is constantly shifting, and the California Geological Survey says Monterey County is particularly susceptible to earthquakes because of its location along the San Andreas Fault system.

The numbers put the shaking in perspective. Magnitude measures the amount of energy released at the source of an earthquake, on a logarithmic scale: each whole number represents a 10-fold increase in amplitude and a 32-fold increase in energy. Intensity, by contrast, measures the effects of an earthquake at a given location, on the Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale, which runs from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). A magnitude 5.1 is strong enough to be felt across a wide area — as far away as San Francisco, in this case — while the damage depends on where it strikes and how deep it is.

The fact that the first magnitude estimate changed — from 5.4 to 5.1 — is part of the story too. Early earthquake figures are preliminary, calculated from the first seismic readings to arrive, and the U.S. Geological Survey revises them as more stations report in. NBC Bay Area’s early report called its figure preliminary and warned that details may change as more information becomes available, a reminder that the first hours after a quake are for checking, not concluding.

Safety guidance for a quake like this is simple and well rehearsed: if you are inside, drop to the floor, take cover under a sturdy table or desk and hold on, protecting your head and torso. If you are outside, move away from buildings, power lines and anything that could fall. If you are driving, pull over, stop, and avoid overpasses, bridges and power lines — then stay calm, stay put until the shaking stops, and check yourself and others for injuries once it has.

Why it matters

A magnitude 5.1 earthquake in Central California matters for two reasons: where it happened, and when. The epicenter sat on one of the most closely watched fault systems in the world, in a county the state’s own geological survey calls particularly susceptible to earthquakes. It was also a reminder that the San Andreas Fault does not wait for a convenient moment — this one struck mid-storm, after flooding, power cuts and toppled trees across the Bay Area.

The response, so far, is the best version of the story. No injuries or major damage were reported in the immediate aftermath, no tsunami warnings were issued, and the early-warning system reached at least one resident before the shaking did. That is the outcome every earthquake drill is designed to produce — and the reason California has invested in ShakeAlert and in telling residents to drop, cover and hold on.

The search surge tells its own story. More than 20,000 searches for “earthquake” piled up in the United States as the news broke, with people checking where the quake struck, how strong it was, and whether it had been felt where they live. In a state built along a fault capable of producing major earthquakes, a moderate quake on a stormy Thursday night was a live rehearsal — and a reminder to check the guidance, secure heavy objects, and know what to do when the ground moves.

For now, the details remain preliminary. The U.S. Geological Survey’s figures may be revised further as more data comes in, and emergency officials are continuing to check for damage. The quake’s message, though, is already clear: a moderate earthquake on the San Andreas can be felt as far away as San Francisco, and the warning an alert can give is worth having.
