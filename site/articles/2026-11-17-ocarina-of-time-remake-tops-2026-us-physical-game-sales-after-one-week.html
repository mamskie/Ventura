Ocarina of Time Remake Tops 2026 US Physical Game Sales After One Week

A 28-year-old adventure has just set a very modern record. After one week on sale, the Nintendo Switch 2 remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is 2026's best-selling physical video game in the United States, passing one million boxed copies in its launch week — the first game to do that since the last Zelda.

The figures come from Circana, which tracks American game sales, and cap a remarkable opening for a remake of a game first released on the Nintendo 64 in 1998. Nintendo had already announced more than 5.2 million copies sold worldwide in the first four days, including 810,000 in Japan — the strongest four-day start for any Nintendo-published Switch 2 game.

The milestone is about more than nostalgia: a remake built by Nintendo's core Zelda team is selling like a brand-new blockbuster — and it raises a question Nintendo has not answered. With the team that makes Zelda busy remaking the past, how long will fans wait for the next original adventure?

What happened in the first week

Ocarina of Time launched worldwide for the Nintendo Switch 2 on November 5, 2026. Within days, Nintendo announced over 5.2 million units sold globally in the first four days, 810,000 of them in Japan — the highest global sales of any Nintendo-published Switch 2 game in its first four days.

Then came the American numbers. Circana's Mat Piscatella reported that after just one week on the market, Ocarina of Time was 2026's best-selling physical video game in the United States. It is the first title to surpass one million physical units in a US launch week since 2023's The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom (data through November 7, 2026).

Nintendo's own rankings tell the same story. According to official data cited by Nintendo Life, Ocarina of Time is already the second-highest selling Switch 2 game globally, ahead of Donkey Kong Bananza at 5.05 million copies. Only launch title Mario Kart World sits ahead, at 16.48 million. The $69.99 physical edition — $59.99 digitally — did not slow it down.

A remake built from the ground up

This is not a simple polish of an old cartridge. The Switch 2 version is a full remake of the 1998 original, in which Link travels between two time periods as Young Link and Adult Link, using different abilities and items in each era. Nintendo rebuilt it with reimagined visuals and enhanced playability, preserving the original experience.

The remake was developed by Nintendo EPD Production Group No. 3, the internal team behind the mainline Zelda games, led by series producer Eiji Aonuma. Remakes are often handed to outside studios; here, the same people who would otherwise be building the next original Zelda spent their time rebuilding a classic instead.

Aonuma has spoken at length about the development, and one detail stands out. He worried that younger developers on the team, who grew up with Breath of the Wild, might not be familiar with the classic Zelda formula — and the remake became the best hands-on practice they could ask for.

The remake also adds a feature the 1998 original could never have offered. When Link plays the ocarina, players can hum the tune into the Switch 2's microphone or play it on a real instrument instead of tapping buttons. The ocarina is a key item — playing it unlocks new locations, calls on characters for advice and changes the day and night cycle — and there are twelve songs to learn. In practice, the feature is mixed: PCWorld found it often needed loud humming or a move closer to the console when docked, and Zelda's Lullaby was often mistaken for Epona's Song, which shares its notes in a different order. Nintendo even sells two playable ocarinas of its own, at $34.99 and $49.99.

Reviewers have been generous. Nintendo Life awarded the remake 9 out of 10, calling it the best way to play one of the most seminal games of all time, and the game sits at 96 on Metacritic after briefly dipping to 95.

Why it matters

The headline number is striking because it cuts against the direction of the games business. Physical discs and cartridges have been losing ground to downloads for years, yet a boxed remake of a 28-year-old game outsold every other physical release of 2026 in its first week. It is a reminder that for the right game, players still want something to hold.

The crown may not last long, and the reason is a technicality. Grand Theft Auto VI arrives later this week, and its retail release is a "code-in-box" edition — a box with a download code, not a disc. Circana counts code-in-box sales as new physical video game sales, so GTA VI could take the title quickly, and it has already smashed pre-order records. For now, though, the record belongs to Hyrule.

For Nintendo, the result proves the remake strategy pays: Ocarina of Time is already the second-biggest Switch 2 game, a week after launch. For Zelda fans, the success comes with a trade-off: the core team spent its time on a remake, and reporting suggests plans for the next mainline game stayed on the back burner until Ocarina was finished. Recent history points to a long wait: six years passed between Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom, and six between Skyward Sword and Breath of the Wild.

That does not mean silence: the series has a long history with smaller-scale games, and Echoes of Wisdom shows how Nintendo fills gaps between big 3D entries. There is also an obvious next step — Majora's Mask, built from Ocarina of Time's assets in the first place, would be a natural follow-up remake.

The search surge is easy to explain — a beloved classic, a record-breaking launch, a game first met in childhood, now selling like a new blockbuster. What Nintendo does with the momentum — Majora's Mask, or a brand-new Hyrule — is the wait that starts now.
