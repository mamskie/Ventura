Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO Just 12 Days After His Resignation

Jensen Huang is back. Twelve days after resigning as chief executive of Nvidia, the co-founder who turned the chipmaker into the engine of the artificial intelligence boom was reinstated on Monday by a board that, only days earlier, had said it had no intention of bringing him back.

The board voted unanimously to restore Huang as president, chief executive and board director, effective immediately. It is the latest and most dramatic turn in a scandal that has shaken the world's most valuable chipmaker, rattled Wall Street, and drawn the White House directly into a corporate boardroom.

"The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead NVIDIA, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers," board chair Harvey C. Jones said in a statement. Huang said he was "honored" to return, and that he resumes the job "with the greatest humility and renewed commitment."

Huang's resignation followed his admission that he had an affair with Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman suspected of being a Chinese spy. The admission set off weeks of turmoil over the relationship and the suspected diversion of Nvidia's advanced chips to China. When Huang posted a message last week saying he would be "honored" to return if the company asked, the board answered that it did not intend to reinstate him. On Monday, it changed its mind.

What happened

The reversal came after a weekend of pressure from the White House. President Donald Trump, who has a close personal relationship with Huang, called for the reinstatement as recently as Sunday night and took credit for the board's decision in an interview with CNBC on Monday.

"I believe my intervention was very important," Trump said. The president said he had spoken to Huang on Sunday, and that his administration had assured Nvidia there were no national security concerns about Huang's return. "The board decided to do what we wanted them to do," he said, declining to say whether he had called into the board meeting.

Vice President JD Vance also pressed the case to the board over the weekend, according to a person familiar with the matter. The White House believed Vance, who worked in Silicon Valley before entering politics, had the stature to help board members overcome their reservations.

Nvidia's own announcement confirmed the government's role. The board said it had received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, was confident that Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk. Trump said Attorney General Todd Blanche had confirmed that the government "does not possess evidence that he has engaged in any unlawful conduct."

Huang returned to Nvidia's headquarters in Silicon Valley on Monday, where employees gathered in the building's atrium and loudly cheered him on, as shown in an employee video broadcast on CNBC. "I am going to be a better CEO the second time around," he said. He thanked Jones and interim chief executive Colette Kress, who returns to her role as chief financial officer.

A deeper look: the market roars back

Wall Street greeted the news with a rally. Shares of Nvidia closed at $165.05, a gain of more than 31%, though still far below the company's all-time high of $239.24 last month. Nvidia's performance helped end a seven-day losing streak that had dragged the S&P 500 into correction territory. The S&P surged 4.7%, the Nasdaq climbed 6.5% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 3%.

Trump acknowledged in the CNBC interview that the stock market's declines last week — one of the worst weeks since the 2008 financial crisis — "probably" influenced his thinking on Huang. He called the reinstatement the "correct and proper decision," writing on social media on Monday that the country "needs Jensen's leadership" at the company "in order to ensure the Success and Prosperity of not only the Company, but the American Economy."

Not everyone was convinced the rally would last. Kathleen Day, a lecturer at Johns Hopkins Carey Business School with expertise in financial crises and corporate governance, said Huang's reinstatement was "just for a short-term boost to the stock market." She equated the move to "wallpapering over mold."

The president's outsized role has also been under scrutiny. "That's what happens when one company becomes 'too big to fail,' and President Trump cares more about Wall Street than American national security," said Nidhi Hegde, executive director of the American Economic Liberties Project. "The administration's interest here isn't a mystery: it needs the AI stocks to keep inflating long enough to take credit for it."

It is not the first time a tech industry leader has been fired over transparency concerns and then quickly rehired after an outpouring of support from staff and investors. Three years ago, OpenAI co-founder Sam Altman was ousted as chief executive after a chaotic power struggle and then reinstated days later after employees revolted.

Why it matters

Huang's return does not close the book on the scandal. The reinstatement does not clear him of further scrutiny, including several congressional investigations over the affair and the suspected diversion of Nvidia's advanced chips to China. Huang is scheduled to sit for an interview next week with three House committees investigating the scandal's implications for national security.

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer said the rush to reinstate Huang "underscores the need for Congress to act," and vowed Democrats would "wield their full power to investigate any wrongdoing."

The Justice Department is conducting a criminal investigation into potential export control violations at Megaspeed, the Singapore-based cloud computing firm where Alice Huang was an executive. On Monday, the Commerce Department added Megaspeed to its trade "entity list," meaning export licenses will likely be denied for U.S. companies doing business with it. Blanche has said the department has not reached any conclusion about whether Alice Huang was a Chinese spy.

Nvidia said a committee of independent directors will continue investigating, with Huang's full support, and the board has formed a National Security and Export Control Committee and is searching for a new chief compliance officer.

Nvidia dominates the market for chips used to train AI models, and its fate is now tied, more visibly than ever, to the politics of the White House — and to a president willing to intervene directly in who runs it. For now, investors are celebrating and Huang is back in the job he built. Whether the return lasts, and what Congress and investigators find next, will decide whether Monday was a comeback or merely a pause.
