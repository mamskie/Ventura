AP Top 25 Arrives a Day Late After Week 11 Shakes the Rankings

College football's most-watched ranking arrived a day late this week, and when it finally landed on Monday afternoon, it told the story of a strange weekend. The Associated Press Top 25, normally released on Sunday afternoons, was pushed back to 2 p.m. EST on Monday after a nationwide aviation shutdown scrambled the sport's schedule — and the poll that emerged showed a top five that barely moved and a bottom half that shifted considerably.

Georgia stayed at No. 1 with 58 first-place votes, followed by unbeaten Utah and Notre Dame; UCLA and Miami rounded out an unchanged top five. Below them, the picture was busier: Wake Forest climbed into the top 10, Iowa and Oregon jumped several places, Kentucky tumbled four spots, Ole Miss slid six, and two new teams — Louisville and Liberty — entered the rankings.

The delay was the story before the rankings were. The AP moved its release by a day because the game between No. 19 Mississippi and Oklahoma had been pushed to Sunday, after the Rebels could not arrange transportation to Norman following the flight stoppage. Releasing on Sunday would have meant asking voters to rank teams before the weekend's slate was complete — so the poll waited, and the search trend followed it.

A poll delayed by grounded planes

The disruption began with a nationwide aviation shutdown that grounded flights on Friday, and college football felt it immediately. Games were bumped to later slots on Saturday to accommodate scrambled travel schedules, and two of the weekend's notable matchups moved outright. Mississippi's trip to Oklahoma, originally a Saturday game, was pushed to Sunday at 1 p.m. EST. The contest between No. 2 Utah and No. 22 Arizona did not kick off until 10:30 p.m. EST on Saturday — a start time that left much of the country asleep before the finish.

The Associated Press said the one-day delay would let voters judge the full slate of games before casting their ballots, with the Mississippi-Oklahoma game the clearest reason: a ranked team was playing a day late, and its result belonged in the poll. It was a rare adjustment, but not an unprecedented one. In 2016, the AP delayed the poll by a day after Hurricane Matthew forced the postponement of the Georgia-South Carolina game.

The delay also explains why "AP poll football" surged as a search on Monday. The poll is a Sunday ritual for college football fans, and its absence left a gap that speculation filled: projections, predicted ballots and live blogs tracking the wait. By Monday afternoon, the audience was already assembled.

The weekend that moved the rankings

Week 11 itself was, in the end, mostly about consolidation at the top. Every team in the top ten of the coaches poll won, and the AP's top five did not change at all. But the games that did produce movement were dramatic enough to carry the weekend.

The most-watched was Utah's 40-39 escape at Arizona. The Utes, ranked No. 2, trailed late in a game that did not start until 10:30 p.m. ET, and needed a 70-yard touchdown pass from Devon Dampier to Creed Whittemore in the final minutes to stay unbeaten at 10-0. Arizona quarterback Noah Fifita threw for 356 yards and three touchdowns — yet the Utes survived, and the poll rewarded survival over style.

Texas produced the weekend's other great escape, erasing a 16-point deficit to beat LSU 22-21 in Baton Rouge. Quarterback Arch Manning threw three interceptions, but his final throw — a pinpoint pass to Raleek Brown with two seconds on the clock — won the game. A win in Baton Rouge, however it is earned, is the kind of result that tends to impress the College Football Playoff selection committee, and the Longhorns stayed at No. 8.

Indiana, meanwhile, beat Southern California 27-13 to keep its own strong season on track, holding at No. 6 in the AP poll. Wake Forest was one of the week's biggest winners, beating SMU by 42 points and climbing from 11th to 10th — the Demon Deacons have now outscored their opponents 218-21 across their last four games, a run of dominance that finally earned them a place in the top ten.

The week's losers slid accordingly. Kentucky, the highest-ranked team to lose on Saturday, fell 21-14 to Florida and dropped from 13th to 17th. Ole Miss slid from 19th to 25th. Houston fell six places, from 14th to 20th, and Southern California slipped from 15th to 18th. Moving the other way, Iowa climbed from 16th to 13th, Oregon from 18th to 14th, Boise State from 17th to 15th and Ohio State from 21st to 16th. Louisville and Liberty entered the poll at 21st and 22nd, the only two newcomers of the week.

The coaches poll, released alongside the AP ranking, told a slightly different story at the margins. Its top four matched the AP — Georgia, Utah, Notre Dame and UCLA — but the coaches had Indiana fifth and Miami sixth, the reverse of the AP order. The coaches kept Ole Miss 16th, nine places above its AP position, and kept LSU and Duke in their top 25; the AP voters left both merely receiving votes and made room instead for Louisville and Liberty. The two polls agree on the shape of the season; they disagree, as usual, on the details.

Why it matters

The poll matters because it is the last widely shared snapshot of the season before the College Football Playoff rankings take over the conversation. The AP Top 25 does not decide who plays for a championship, but it frames the debate: which teams look like contenders, which look like pretenders, and how much a single weekend can change either judgment.

This week's snapshot is unusually stable at the top. Georgia, Utah and UCLA are a combined 29-1, and Utah's 10-0 record leaves the Utes, in Bleacher Report's words, with one foot in the playoff field. Notre Dame and Miami, both 9-1, sit close behind. With the regular season winding down, the teams at the top increasingly need only to avoid a stumble — while the teams below them need a statement.

The delay is also a reminder of how much of the sport depends on things that have nothing to do with football: a flight stoppage moved kickoff times, pushed a game to Sunday and forced the most established ranking in the sport to wait a day. The poll adapted, and the weekend's results still counted in full. But for a day, the most reliable appointment in college football was missing — and millions of searches showed how many people noticed.
