Thanksgiving Nor'easter Knocks Out Power for Thousands as Dominion Braces for More

Thousands of Dominion Energy customers in Virginia and North Carolina lost power on Wednesday night as a nor'easter rolled into the region on the eve of Thanksgiving, and the utility warned that more outages were likely as the storm's strongest winds arrived. The blackouts turned "Dominion power outage" into one of the most searched topics in the United States overnight, as families checked whether their holiday plans would survive the storm.

By 10:30 p.m. on Wednesday, the outages were spread across the map. In Virginia, 490 customers were out in Suffolk, 471 in York County, 294 in James City County, about 150 in Norfolk and 140 in Newport News, according to 13NEWSNOW. Across the state line in North Carolina, 1,744 customers were without power in Chowan County, with about 56 out in Currituck County and 32 in Perquimans County.

The numbers were still climbing as the storm moved in. An outage map embedded in the 13NEWSNOW report showed 4,542 customers without power across 124 projects, a snapshot of a grid under strain before the worst of the wind had arrived. Dominion had spent the day preparing for trees and branches brought down onto power lines.

What happened

The nor'easter arrived just in time for Thanksgiving, bringing the ingredients Dominion fears most — sustained wind strong enough to throw debris into power lines, and wet, heavy conditions that make trees more likely to fall. In central Virginia, the storm was expected to bring winds of 30 to 40 mph, according to Dominion spokesperson Craig Carper, speaking to WRIC ABC 8News.

"The greatest potential for outages is from things striking power lines," Carper said. "It's more likely to happen during periods of heavy wind." Wind advisories and high wind warnings were in place until 4 p.m. on Thanksgiving Day, according to WRIC's StormTracker8 team — meaning the danger window stretched across the holiday itself, not just the night before.

The outages were not confined to one corner of the map. Suffolk and York County led the Virginia counts, while the largest single cluster was across the border in Chowan County, North Carolina, where 1,744 customers lost power. The spread showed how wide the storm's footprint was — from the Chesapeake Bay communities of Hampton Roads to the rural counties of northeastern North Carolina.

Dominion had been watching the storm for days. Ahead of its arrival, crews were preparing for downed trees that bring down power lines, and the utility staged teams across the region so they could move quickly once conditions allowed. "We've got crews that are staged and ready to go if needed," Dominion spokesperson Bonita Billingsley told 13NEWSNOW.

The utility had a recent lesson to draw on. Two months ago, another nor'easter brought salt spray contamination that knocked out power for thousands of customers. Dominion said it was prepared for that risk this time, too. "Any time we have a wind event, there's potential for salt spray, but we're not projected to see any as of right now," Dominion's Jackson said.

How Dominion is responding

The scale of the response matched the timing. Carper said hundreds of line crews were on call around the clock for Thanksgiving, ready to respond to any issues, and that regional officers and operations centers were fully staffed for the storm. Dominion trucks prepared for Thursday carried the same equipment used for daily construction and repairs year-round. "They're ready to go at any time," Carper said.

The preparation reflected a hard truth about storm response: crews can only work so fast once the wind picks up, and the safest repairs often have to wait until conditions ease. Staging crews in advance — rather than calling them in after the damage — is how a utility keeps restoration times down when outages number in the thousands and the calls keep coming.

Dominion's message to customers was as much about preparation as repair. The utility said customers did not have to wait for a complete outage to report a problem — even flickering lights should be reported through the company's website or mobile app, and up-to-date outage information was available on Dominion's online outage map.

For households, Carper offered a short checklist: charge phones in advance, keep snacks and non-perishable food on hand, and make sure medications are within reach. It was practical advice for a holiday built around a hot meal — and a reminder that a Thanksgiving outage is not only an inconvenience but a disruption to the day's cooking, heating and travel plans.

"We know this is coming and we're prepared for it," Carper said. "We're not expecting this to be a storm for the history books, but we recognize that this is Thanksgiving Day and people have plans." The wind, he added, "can do a fair amount during that period of time."

Why it matters

The storm matters first because of when it arrived. Thanksgiving is one of the few days when nearly every household is cooking, hosting or travelling at once, and a power outage on that day lands differently from one on an ordinary Tuesday. A dark kitchen means a turkey that cannot finish cooking; a cold house means guests who cannot stay; a dead phone means a family that cannot coordinate.

It also matters because the outages arrived before the storm's peak. The counts reported at 10:30 p.m. on Wednesday were recorded while wind advisories and high wind warnings were still in force until 4 p.m. on Thanksgiving — so the worst of the wind, and the most likely window for new outages, was still ahead when thousands were already in the dark.

The search surge itself is part of the story. "Dominion power outage" became one of the most searched phrases in the United States overnight, a sign of how many people were checking the same question at the same time: is my power out, and when will it return? In a storm, the outage map becomes the most visited page in the region — and the answers it gives shape the holiday for thousands of families.

The broader lesson is about a grid that faces the same test every year. Nor'easters are a known hazard for Virginia's coast, bringing the same combination of wind, water and falling trees. Dominion's answer — staged crews, full staffing, and a plea to report flickering lights early — is built from the last storm's lessons, including the salt spray failure two months ago. Whether it holds up will be measured in how fast the lights come back on, and in how many Thanksgiving tables stay lit.
