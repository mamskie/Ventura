Avengers: Doomsday Trailer Expected Monday as Joe Russo Explains the Year-Long Tease

The wait for Avengers: Doomsday may be measured in hours. Hayley Atwell, who returns as Peggy Carter, told fans at a New York autograph signing on Friday night that a new trailer is coming on Monday. “There's a new trailer dropping on Monday… am I allowed to have said that?” she said — and within hours the remark had set off a weekend of feverish expectation online.

The timing fits a pattern Marvel has been building for a year. Hours before Atwell spoke, some 7,000 miles away in Qatar, co-director Joe Russo told an audience at the Doha Industry Days that fresh material was on the way before the film opens on December 18, urging fans to keep checking their feeds. The search surge says the strategy is working: “marvel avengers doomsday trailer” led US Google trending searches over the weekend.

What happened

Atwell's slip was the spark, but not the only signal. Disney has also announced a November 30 sneak peek during its Holiday Spectacular on ABC — believed to be a different tease. Together, the two dates suggest Marvel is about to enter its busiest stretch of promotion yet.

The latest trailer already hints at the scale. Marvel released a 30-second “Return” promo uniting five distinct teams against Robert Downey Jr.'s Doctor Doom, flipping the Marvel Cinematic Universe's end-credits tagline to ask fans to return to theaters. The friction starts on Earth-616, where Sam Wilson's Avengers and Yelena's New Avengers are divided — a split rooted in Baron Zemo's victory in Captain America: Civil War.

Marvel Studios president Kevin Feige confirmed Doomsday will explore the consequences of that fracture, navigating competing claims to the Avengers name after years with no organized team. Sam Wilson, played by Anthony Mackie, will lead not just his own Avengers — Ant-Man, Shang-Chi, Falcon and Thor among them — but all five teams that must stand against Doom: five legacies, forced to coexist, standing between Doom and the Multiverse.

A deeper look: four teasers, one story told in public

The Monday trailer, if it arrives as Atwell suggested, is the latest chapter in a rollout Russo described in Qatar. Speaking in a fireside chat at the Doha Industry Days, he said the four teasers released so far — ahead of an official trailer this summer — had played an integral role in shaping the final version of the film. The idea, he said, was to start the story a year early, dropping clues in the year leading up to the movie.

The clues arrived in character-sized pieces. The first, on December 23, 2025, showed Steve Rogers, played by Chris Evans, returning home, unpacking his Captain America suit and holding a baby. A November 30 teaser showed Thor, played by Chris Hemsworth, praying to Odin for strength. A January 6 drop featured the X-Men, and a January 13 teaser the Wakandans and the Fantastic Four. Together, Russo said, they add up to two and a half hours on December 18.

The fragments also carry the plot's fault lines. The Fantastic Four sit at the center because of their personal connection to Doom — Marvel has confirmed Downey's Doom comes from Earth-828, and the post-credits scene of The Fantastic Four: First Steps suggested he may be about to abduct Franklin Richards, the child of Reed and Sue Richards. The Wakandans, led by Shuri and King M'Baku alongside Namor and the Talokanil, are the bridge between the teams. And the X-Men are the wild card: footage has shown Patrick Stewart's Charles Xavier watching a world destroyed in real time, with Ian McKellen's Magneto beside him, and rumors suggest this team has made sacrifices that leave them morally ambiguous — even fighting the Avengers at times.

The New Avengers are more than a second string. The team has spent 14 months protecting New York since assembling at the end of Thunderbolts, with Florence Pugh's Yelena as leader and Sentry's unstable power making the group Earth-616's biggest wild card. That instability is the point: in a film about a collapsing Multiverse, the heroes cannot be sure of each other.

Why it matters

Russo's explanation is unusually candid about how much the audience has changed since he last directed an Avengers film. “I have to work extra hard to subvert your expectations and be aware of who you are as a viewer and how you're changing,” he said — a landscape transformed since Avengers: Infinity War and Avengers: Endgame in 2018 and 2019. The pandemic pushed audiences toward social media and streaming while cinemas suffered, and now, he said, that trend appears to be reversing. “You have to be a futurist to try to predict where the market is going.”

The teasers, in that light, are not just marketing — they are research. “I have to interact with the fans in order to understand what is moving them, what is working for them,” Russo said, adding that the speed of online conversation is far faster than it was in 2018 and 2019. Releasing the story in pieces lets Marvel watch what fans track and what they miss, and adjust. “It's slowly investing them emotionally,” he said. “But I'm also getting feedback as that's happening and we can make adjustments as we go.” For a story he describes as very complicated, that feedback loop matters: if fans take the wrong idea from a crumb of footage, the next crumb can correct it.

The film itself exists because of a similarly slow process. Russo said he was initially reluctant to return, still recovering from Infinity War and Endgame, and that it was Downey who planted the seed — first by asking over dinner whether Russo would come back, then by revealing he wanted to play Victor von Doom. The pitch clicked, Russo said, because of the symmetry: the greatest hero's actor becoming the greatest villain. But he still said no — there was no story.

The story arrived from longtime collaborator Steve McFeely, who proposed picking up the cameras at the end of Endgame and asking what happens in the very next scene. The premise asks what if happiness is finite — what if one character's happy ending has to be taken from someone else. “That's where I went, 'Right, I know what to do with this,'” he said. “The idea that like there was a cost to a happy ending.” That cost is now the engine of the film, and if Atwell is right, the next piece of it lands on Monday.
