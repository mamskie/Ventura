Kent State's Walk-Off Touchdown Overturned in 17-12 Loss to Eastern Michigan

Kent State's season ended in the cruellest fashion imaginable on Tuesday night, when a last-second touchdown that would have beaten Eastern Michigan was wiped out on replay — and a slow-motion look at the play suggests the officials got it wrong.

The Golden Flashes trailed 17-12 and faced fourth-and-9 from the Eastern Michigan 14 with the clock nearly spent, when quarterback Ethan Thanthanavong lofted a pass towards the back-right corner of the end zone. Wide receiver Wayne Harris leapt, secured the ball and was ruled in for a walk-off score — until a review lasting several minutes overturned the call, turning the play into a turnover on downs and handing the Eagles the win.

The reversal did more than decide a game between two teams already out of bowl contention. It reignited the oldest argument in college football — what exactly counts as a catch — and left the Mid-American Conference facing a second replay controversy in a single season.

What happened

The final Tuesday night MACtion game of the season, played in Ypsilanti, began at a sprint. Eastern Michigan scored a touchdown on its first offensive drive, with Amareon Blue breaking free for a 61-yard score in the opening minute on his way to 137 rushing yards. Kent State answered, and the teams traded touchdowns before half-time. Brendyn Smith scored both of the Golden Flashes' touchdowns, one on a 17-yard catch and the other on a 100-yard kickoff return — but Kent State failed the two-point conversion after each score and went into the break trailing 14-12.

The second half was, by one account, a total snoozer — until it wasn't. With just under two minutes remaining, Eastern Michigan added a field goal to stretch the lead to 17-12. The kick, a 28-yarder from Rudy Kessinger, was set up by Bryce Llewellyn's second interception of Thanthanavong, returned to the Kent State 11.

Kent State still had one last chance. The Golden Flashes drove 54 yards on six plays in one minute and 55 seconds to set up fourth-and-9 from the 14 with seconds left. Thanthanavong, a former Division III third-string quarterback, went to the air — and Harris appeared to win the game as time expired, making the catch in the back corner of the end zone and getting a foot down in bounds.

Or so it seemed. The officials spent several minutes under the hood before ruling the pass incomplete, and put one second back on the clock so the Eagles could kneel out the win. Harris, a 5-foot-9 redshirt sophomore from Charleston, West Virginia, had caught five passes for 125 yards on the night, including a 47-yarder. Thanthanavong finished with 234 passing yards and a touchdown, but his two interceptions proved costly.

The call under the microscope

A slowed-down clip of the catch circulating on X makes Kent State's case. Harris has the ball in his hands when his left foot lands in the end zone. The football only shifts after he hits the turf, and it never comes loose.

The referees saw it differently. They determined that Harris did not maintain possession of the football through the ground — that the ball was still bouncing around in his arms, and that he never completed the catch. The ruling stood: incomplete.

That reasoning sits uneasily with how college replay is supposed to work. The NCAA's replay manual requires indisputable video evidence to change a call made on the field. A review that drags on for several minutes is a strong sign the evidence was anything but indisputable — and on the available angles, the case for overturning the touchdown looked thin.

When asked about his conversations with the officials, Kent State head coach Mark Carney offered little. "Control of the ball and where his foot was, there was an explanation," he said.

The reversal also cost Harris a milestone. The redshirt sophomore finished the season with 60 catches for 988 yards and seven touchdowns — so the 14-yard score would have pushed him past 1,000 yards for the year, a landmark instead recorded as an incompletion.

This is the Mid-American Conference's second replay mess in a season already full of them. In September, a Big Ten replay crew put one second back on the clock at Michigan, and the Wolverines scored on the final play to beat Western Michigan 13-12. The MAC formally appealed and asked that Western Michigan be recognised as the winner, but the NCAA said it cannot overturn the result.

The two episodes share an uncomfortable pattern: a long review, a changed call, and a result that cannot be undone. Replay was introduced to correct clear mistakes, not to re-referee close plays from the booth — and when a crew needs several minutes to find a reason to overturn, the doubt itself is the answer.

Why it matters

For Kent State, the call was the difference between a fourth win and a bitter ending. The Golden Flashes finished 3-9 overall and 2-6 in the MAC after losing their last four games, and the defeat will linger precisely because it was decided by officials rather than by the players. Harris, at least, returns as the top target in the passing game — a consolation, but a real one.

For Eastern Michigan, the win closed a 5-7 season (4-4 in the MAC) and snapped a four-game losing streak. It was a victory earned on the field for 59 minutes and preserved in the replay booth in the final one.

Neither team entered the week with a bowl game at stake. They were playing for pride, in the last game of the season for both sides, and there is nothing left for Kent State to appeal. That is part of what makes the ending sting: the season is over, the record is final, and the argument about the catch will outlast both.

The wider question is whether the sport's replay system still serves the game it was built to protect. Every fan who watched the slow-motion clip can see what Harris did — secure the ball, get a foot down, hold on until the turf intervened. Until officials apply the indisputable-evidence standard as written, endings like this one will keep arriving, and the phrase "what is a catch" will keep having no good answer.
