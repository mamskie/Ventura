Hackers Breach the U.S. Air Force's Flagship Cloud One Computing Platform

Hackers have breached the U.S. Air Force’s flagship cloud computing platform, three people familiar with the matter told Reuters, in an intrusion that could jeopardise a vast swathe of sensitive tactical, personnel and logistics data. The breach at Cloud One was discovered within the past month or so, during work to upgrade the system — and its discovery has already disrupted operations across the platform.

Reuters could not establish the identity of the hackers, the full scope of the intrusion or whether any data had been stolen. The Air Force declined to discuss the incident in detail, telling Reuters it “does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems,” and that it “continuously take[s] prudent measures to protect Department (of the Air Force) information, systems and mission capabilities.”

What happened inside Cloud One

Launched in 2017, Cloud One was built to be a one-stop shop for the Air Force’s computing needs, combining the resources of four operators — Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet — to power scores of applications, including ones that manage personnel data and pay, logistics and maintenance.

The systems running on it are not back-office curiosities. Among them is the Air Force’s Cloud-Based Command and Control, which collates air defence radar and other data feeds from across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, which tracks bombs and missiles across more than 200 U.S. Air Force locations around the world. Two of Reuters’ sources described Cloud One as among the U.S. military’s biggest cloud platforms. “A breach here is a big deal,” one of them said.

SAIC was the prime contractor for Cloud One until 2025, when Leidos took over. SAIC did not respond to a request for comment; Leidos referred questions to the Air Force. Investigators have called in Cyber Command, the U.S. military’s cyber force, to assess the damage. Cyber Command did not respond to requests for comment.

One source told Reuters the intruders exploited a software vulnerability to reach the platform’s control plane, likening that access to a universal remote: in theory, the hackers could have used it to check the operational status of military aircraft or the movement of spare parts — though it was unclear whether they did so. Reuters could not identify the specific vulnerability the hackers exploited.

The discovery froze work across the platform

The discovery of the hack led to a range of disruption, according to the sources. Efforts to migrate back-office applications onto Cloud One — a longstanding military priority — have been paused. Updates to applications already running on the platform were blocked, leaving Air Force personnel unable to apply fixes just as a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities was disclosed across the technology industry. A fifth source said some users had their administrator rights revoked. All five sources spoke on condition of anonymity.

The timing makes the freeze worse than an inconvenience. With patches blocked, the systems that run pay, personnel records, logistics and maintenance sit exposed to newly disclosed flaws while the Air Force works out what the intruders touched. Gizmodo, reporting on the Reuters account, noted that personnel have been unable to apply patches for “a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities” as the military works to mitigate the hack and assess its damage.

The story travelled fast. The search phrase “hackers breach air force” surged on Google Trends in the United States on Friday morning, a measure of how quickly news of a military cyber incident reaches beyond defence circles — and of how little the public has been told so far.

Why this breach matters

The breach lands in the middle of a grim run for U.S. government cybersecurity. According to Reuters, the government is already dealing with the fallout from at least six other recently disclosed cyber incidents, including two at the FBI, a previous hack of Pentagon data, a recent leak of Department of Homeland Security personnel information, a breach affecting at least two spy agencies and a hack at the Treasury Department.

Gizmodo’s account adds more names to that list: a September breach compromised FBI officials’ personal details, and more recently the systems of the Central Intelligence Agency and the Pentagon’s National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency were compromised, apparently exposing non-classified data. The outlet also notes a string of high-profile technology problems this month — a cyberattack aimed at the Algonquin Dam in Illinois, an outage at the National Security Agency’s internal messaging service and a nationwide Federal Aviation Administration ground stop of flights — alongside a prolonged Microsoft 365 outage, login problems at the biggest U.S. banks and days of disruption at Allstate.

Many of those outages and service issues were attributed to maintenance rather than to cyberattacks, Gizmodo noted. But the pattern has officials warning that more disruption is coming: earlier this week, U.S. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton told CNN that disruptions like these will continue as the nation’s “dated” digital infrastructure is upgraded.

The anxiety is sharpened by the rise of AI-powered intrusions, a particular problem for cloud-based networks because AI models can swiftly detect weaknesses across wide surface areas, Reuters reported. Cloud One has long been in hackers’ crosshairs: in a 2023 commentary carried by the online publication FedScoop, the platform’s leadership touted what it called a “100% success rate” in beating back what it described as a “staggering” number of attempts to penetrate the platform. “There is no room for error when it comes to security,” the leadership wrote.

For now, the central questions have no answers: who got in, how far they reached and whether anything was taken. Until Cyber Command’s assessment is complete, the Air Force’s flagship cloud — the system meant to modernise how the service stores data, pays its people and tracks its weapons — is operating under a cloud of its own.
