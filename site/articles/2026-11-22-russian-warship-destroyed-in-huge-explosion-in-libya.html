Russian Warship Destroyed in Huge Explosion at Libya's Benghazi Naval Base

A Russian warship has been destroyed in a huge explosion at the Libyan port of Benghazi, according to Naval News, which reports that the Steregushchiy-class corvette Merkury was engulfed in flames on Sunday and is almost certainly a total loss. The blast struck an ammunition depot inside the city's naval base, setting off a fire that brought emergency crews to the military facility in the early hours of the morning.

The story surged to the top of search trends across the United States on Sunday, with “russian navy vessel fire benghazi” leading Google's US trending searches. But for all the attention, the most basic question — what caused the explosion — remains unanswered. Neither Libyan authorities nor Russian officials have said how the blast happened, or whether anyone was hurt.

What happened

The explosion hit the naval base in Benghazi, the port city in eastern Libya, on Sunday morning. According to local reports cited by Ahram Online, a fire broke out inside an ammunition depot at the base, and civil defence teams were sent to the military facility to contain the blaze. Al-Masar TV, a Libyan news channel, cited local civil defence forces as saying response teams had been deployed after the reported blast. Libya Alhadath TV said civil defence urged people in the area to stay away, and later reported that the fire had been put out.

Middle East Eye, citing local emergency authorities via Al-Masar TV, reported the same sequence: an explosion inside the depot on Sunday morning, emergency teams deployed to contain the resulting fire, and no word from the authorities on the cause or on casualties. As of the latest updates, officials had still not disclosed either.

Naval News, which first identified the vessel involved, reported that a large explosion in the port indicates a Russian missile corvette has almost certainly been destroyed. The ship, provisionally identified as the Project 20380 Steregushchiy-class corvette Merkury, was engulfed in flames, the outlet said, although it is not yet clear whether it sank at its berth or was simply burnt out. Several sources seen by Naval News confirmed the incident, and the Merkury could be seen on fire in multiple satellite images, the outlet reported.

The Merkury had been at the port for several days when the explosion occurred, according to Naval News. The vessel is nominally part of Russia's Black Sea Fleet, but it has been operating out of Northern Fleet bases in the Arctic because of the war in the Black Sea. It was escorting a convoy of Russian government-linked ships that are used for carrying arms exports, the outlet reported.

A deeper look: a corvette caught in a shadow war at sea

The Merkury is not the first Russian warship to be hit far from the Black Sea. Its sistership, the Boikiy, was seriously damaged on 3 June 2026 by Ukrainian aerial drones in the Veleshinsky drydock at Kronstadt, near St. Petersburg, according to Naval News. That attack set off a powerful explosion and a huge fire that heavily damaged the ship's superstructure.

Naval News notes that Ukraine has been reported to be behind a number of successful attacks on Russian ships in the Mediterranean, and the Benghazi explosion fits a pattern that has been building for two years. On 23 December 2024, the Russian government-linked ship Ursa Major was sunk, likely by limpet mines. On 3 March 2026, the liquefied natural gas tanker Arctic Metagaz was destroyed off the Libyan coast, likely by uncrewed surface vessels.

It is important to be clear about what is not known. Naval News states plainly that exactly how the Benghazi explosion came about has not been reported. No group has claimed responsibility, and no official account of the cause has been published. The identification of the ship itself is provisional, and the question of whether the Merkury sank or burnt out at its berth is still open. What is established is the blast, the fire, and the satellite images of the vessel burning.

The setting matters too. Benghazi sits in eastern Libya, a country that has been beset by violence and instability since the overthrow of Muammar Gaddafi in 2011, as Ahram Online's report notes. Libya remains divided between the UN-recognized government in Tripoli, led by Prime Minister Abdulhamid Dbeibah, and a rival administration in the east backed by military commander Khalifa Haftar. A Russian warship docking in an eastern Libyan port, escorting a convoy of government-linked vessels, is part of a much larger contest over the Mediterranean — and over who supplies whom.

Why it matters

First, if the loss is confirmed, it would be a significant blow to a navy that has already lost ships in the Black Sea and is now operating far from its home waters. A modern missile corvette is not a ship a fleet gives up lightly, and the Merkury was on an active escort mission, not sitting in reserve. Its destruction, if confirmed, would underline how far the war at sea has spread from Ukraine's coast.

Second, the incident raises the stakes for every Russian-linked vessel moving through the Mediterranean. The Ursa Major and the Arctic Metagaz were civilian or government-linked ships; the Merkury is a warship, and it was hit while alongside in a friendly port. That suggests the threat is not limited to open water, and that ports once considered safe are now part of the battlespace.

Third, the explosion puts Libya back in the spotlight as a theatre where outside powers operate in the open. The country's division means foreign warships can dock in the east while the internationally recognized government sits in Tripoli, and incidents like this one expose how little control any single authority has over what happens in its ports.

For now, the picture is incomplete. Satellite images show the Merkury on fire, emergency teams have contained the blaze at the depot, and the authorities have said nothing about the cause or the casualties. The answers will come from official statements, further imagery, and the wreck itself — whether it lies on the harbor floor or as a burnt-out hull at its berth. Until then, the explosion in Benghazi stands as the latest, and most dramatic, episode in a quiet war being fought across the Mediterranean.
