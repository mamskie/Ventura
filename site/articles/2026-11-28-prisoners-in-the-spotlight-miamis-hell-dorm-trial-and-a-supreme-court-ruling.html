Prisoners in the Spotlight: Miami’s ‘Hell Dorm’ Trial and a Supreme Court Ruling

"Prisoners" is one of the most searched words in the United States this week, and the reason is not one story but two courtroom dramas. In Miami, a federal judge is hearing testimony about a South Florida prison dormitory so hot that the men who live there call it the "Hell Dorm". In Washington, the Supreme Court has settled a long-running fight over whether prisoners can win shorter sentences now that Congress has decided the punishments they received were too harsh. Together, the two cases ask the same uncomfortable question: how does the justice system treat the people already inside it?

What happened

In Miami, the trial opened this week before U.S. District Judge Kathleen M. Williams, who is hearing the case without a jury. The prisoners, represented by the Florida Justice Institute, want the court to order changes to the heat and ventilation at Dade Correctional Institution, a concrete prison in Homestead, south of Miami. They are not seeking money. The Florida Department of Corrections disputes that conditions there create the substantial risk of serious harm the prisoners claim, and argues officials have taken reasonable measures.

The most striking testimony came from Jeremy Ingram, 43, now held at Avon Park Correctional Institution. Ingram spent 15 months in Iraq as an Army radar operator, in temperatures he recalled reaching 115 to 120 degrees. Asked whether he would rather endure that dry heat in full combat uniform or the humid heat inside a South Florida prison dormitory, he chose battle. "I would much rather be in Iraq," he testified on Wednesday. In Iraq he could retreat to an air-conditioned bunk area or take a cold shower; inside H Dorm at Dade, the walls stayed hot into the night, and he showered five or six times a day to cool down — but the relief lasted only minutes. "Maybe five minutes," he said.

In Washington, the Supreme Court ruled in Rutherford v. United States, a case about two men sentenced under a federal gun law before the FIRST STEP Act of 2018 changed the rules. Daniel Rutherford received a 32-year minimum sentence and Johnnie Carter a 57-year minimum, because prosecutors stacked multiple counts under the same statute. The 2018 law stopped that practice. Had the two men been sentenced after it took effect, their minimums would have been 14 years and 21 years. Writing for the majority, Justice Amy Coney Barrett held that the gap did not count as an "extraordinary and compelling" reason for resentencing, because nonretroactive changes to sentencing laws are "the norm". Justice Sonia Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justices Ketanji Brown Jackson and Elena Kagan.

A closer look at the evidence

The numbers behind the Miami case are stark. Sensors installed in the dormitories in 2025 recorded an overall median heat index of 98.3 degrees and a maximum of 119.4 degrees, according to University of California, Berkeley professor Stefano Schiavon. In the second wing of "Hell Dorm", the heat index stayed above 108 degrees for 34 consecutive hours between 28 and 30 July 2025, and indoor heat indexes typically peaked at around 10 p.m. The state accepts the raw sensor data as authentic but disputes what it proves.

The lawsuit covers all eight main housing dormitories at Dade, and the parties agree none of them has air conditioning. The cells in H Dorm have small perforated metal openings instead of windows that open, and the buildings have little insulation and few shade trees. There are no fans inside the cells, and prisoners shaped cardboard from boxes of soup bought through the canteen into makeshift guides to push what little air there was toward their bunks. Ice arrived about three times a day, shared among 82 people in each wing, and could run out within 15 minutes. Maintenance records showed an average of well over 100 ventilation-related work orders a year.

The human cost was described by Derrick Leonard Young, 66, who is blind and uses a wheelchair and has lived in all eight of Dade's dormitories. He told the court about soaked clothing and sheets, itching rashes, breathing difficulties and dizziness he associated with the heat. Because he needed someone else to push his wheelchair, he sometimes had to offer food from his canteen in exchange for a trip to collect his medication, and when he could not find help he sometimes missed the medication window.

The state's medical expert, Dr Christopher Wenger, testified that the evidence did not prove heat had worsened the prisoners' cardiac conditions — but conceded he had never visited Dade and had spent less than 30 minutes reviewing the temperature data.

The sentencing case has a history of its own. The FIRST STEP Act, signed by President Donald Trump in December 2018, changed federal sentencing in several ways, including the gun law at issue in Rutherford. It passed 87 to 12 in the Senate and 358 to 36 in the House. Yet most of its sentencing changes were not retroactive — a puzzle, because the same law did apply the shorter crack-cocaine penalties of the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010 retroactively, a provision that benefited about 4,000 prisoners. The lower courts were split over whether that gap could count as an "extraordinary and compelling" reason for compassionate release. Rutherford has now resolved the split against the prisoners.

Why it matters

The two stories land in the same week, but they point at different parts of the same problem. In Miami, the question is whether the state has done enough about a danger it can measure: sensors, work orders and medical records all describe the same hot, airless buildings, and the judge must now decide whether that exposure, and the officials' response to it, violates constitutional and disability law. No ruling has been issued yet.

In Washington, the question was whether the law should correct itself for people already sentenced, and the Supreme Court's answer was no. The majority's reasoning was that Congress made a deliberate choice to apply its reforms prospectively, and that a deliberate choice cannot itself be a compelling reason to undo it. The practical effect is that thousands of prisoners will keep serving sentences that Congress now regards as too long.

That is why the search term keeps climbing. Americans are not only following a trial or a ruling; they are watching two institutions decide how much the past should weigh on people who are still living with it. For the men at Dade, the trial will decide whether the state must cool the dormitories they sleep in tonight. For Rutherford, Carter and others like them, the ruling means the calendar, not the new law, decides when they go home.
