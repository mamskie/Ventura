GTA 6 Rakes In $3.79 Billion on Day One — the Biggest Entertainment Launch Ever

Grand Theft Auto 6 is out, and it has already made history. Rockstar Games said Friday that the game sold 32.5 million copies before release and generated $3.79 billion in retail sales on its first day — a figure the studio says makes it the biggest entertainment launch in history, larger than any film, album or video game before it.

The numbers beat even the rosiest forecasts. Analysts had expected about 20 million pre-orders, and the research firm SensorTower estimated 24.5 million preorders and $2.36 billion in revenue across PlayStation and Xbox. Rockstar's own tally left both estimates behind — and it did so in a single day, on a game that had already been delayed twice. "It is with immense gratitude that we enter a new chapter of Rockstar Games with the release of Grand Theft Auto VI," Sam Houser, Rockstar's president and founder, said in a statement. "We are incredibly humbled by the response from players around the world and we couldn't be more excited to share this moment with you."

What happened

GTA 6 arrived on November 19, thirteen years after Grand Theft Auto 5, and players showed up in numbers the industry has never seen. The game sells for $79.99, or $99.99 for the Ultimate Edition — a higher price than the $60 tag GTA 5 carried in 2013 — and demand still overwhelmed supply in places. The PlayStation 5 Pro, widely described as offering the best experience for the game, sold out at retailers, and the ongoing memory shortage has been pushing up prices for consoles and PCs alike, leaving room for resellers to charge far above retail.

The scale becomes clearer next to the records it broke. Avengers: Endgame and Avatar, the highest-grossing films ever, each took in an estimated $2.9 billion — over their entire theatrical runs. GTA 6 passed that in a day. Taylor Swift's Eras tour grossed $2.2 billion, but that took nearly two years. Even the industry's own yardstick looks small: Cyberpunk 2077, the previous standard-bearer for pre-release hype, drew just over 8 million preorders in 2020.

GTA 5 remains the bigger game over time — it has shipped 235 million units since 2013 — but its first day looks modest beside its successor: a little over $800 million from about 11 million units, and just over $1 billion across its first three days. The comparison cuts both ways: GTA 6's launch is unprecedented, and it still has years of sales ahead of it.

The launch has not been entirely smooth. A database for the Xbox build of the game, reported to be 209GB, appears to have leaked, and modders and pirates have been eager to bring the game to PC unofficially — a particular problem because Rockstar has only released the game for consoles so far, with a PC release rumored for the first quarter of next year. Meanwhile, Rockstar is reportedly targeting 2027 for the game's online multiplayer mode, which, if GTA 5's long-lived GTA Online is any guide, is where the game's real long-term money will be made.

A deeper look: the workers behind the record

The record-breaking launch is happening against an uneasy backdrop inside the studio that made it. More than 1,600 Rockstar workers in the UK are voting between November 12 and 26 on whether to recognize their branch of the Independent Workers' Union of Great Britain. In an op-ed for The Guardian, Nestor Uriarte, one of the UK staff involved in the union effort, wrote that it "says a lot about the industry that even my colleagues and I, working on the biggest game of all time, are worried about getting laid off."

The worry has a recent cause. Rockstar laid off more than 30 developers in October 2025, most of whom were trying to organize its workers. The studio says it dismissed them for sharing confidential information; the workers say they were fired for union activity, and their case has played out in an employment tribunal in the run-up to the game's release. Rockstar has said it will accept the result of the vote. "We go where the team goes," Jennifer Kolbe, Rockstar's head of publishing, told Bloomberg in October. "So if they want a union, they will have a union."

There is an irony in the numbers: the game reportedly cost between $1 billion and $1.5 billion to develop — itself a staggering sum — and it earned more than twice that in a single day. Yet the people who built it are the ones wondering whether their jobs will survive the celebration. The pattern is familiar across the tech industry, where companies have cut staff even while reporting record revenue. The union vote, and the tribunal, will decide whether Rockstar's workers get a formal say in what comes next.

Why it matters

First, GTA 6 resets the ceiling for what entertainment can earn — and what it can charge. A $79.99 standard price was a gamble; the first-day numbers suggest players will pay it for a game they have waited 13 years to play. Every publisher watching will take note, and the $80 game may soon be the norm rather than the exception.

Second, the launch shows how much of the industry now depends on a handful of blockbusters. One game generated more revenue in a day than most studios earn in a decade, while smaller games fight for attention. That concentration brings risk as well as reward: years of work, a billion-dollar budget, and two delays all rode on a single release date — and this time, the bet paid off.

Third, the contrast between the sales figures and the union vote is the story that will outlast launch week. A game can make $3.79 billion in a day and still leave its makers worried about their jobs. Whether the vote ends in recognition, and how the tribunal rules, will shape Rockstar's future as surely as any sales record — and other studios, watching a workforce organize at the peak of success, will be watching too.

For now, though, the record stands. Thirteen years after GTA 5, its successor did in one day what the biggest films in history needed entire theatrical runs to achieve. The question now is not whether GTA 6 is a hit — that is settled — but what Rockstar does with the moment: the PC version, the online mode, and the people who built it all.
