Huawei Convicted in US Criminal Trial: Guilty on 10 Counts, Cleared of Racketeering

A federal jury in Brooklyn convicted Huawei Technologies on Tuesday of bank fraud and conspiring to steal trade secrets, handing the US Justice Department a landmark victory in its eight-year case against the Chinese telecom giant — while acquitting the company of the racketeering charge at the centre of the prosecution's case.

After a trial lasting more than two months, the jury found Huawei guilty on 10 of 12 criminal counts. It cleared the company of racketeering conspiracy, the charge alleging Huawei operated as a criminal enterprise over two decades.

Prosecutors accused Huawei of lying, cheating and stealing to gain any competitive advantage — deceiving banks about its business in Iran and taking trade secrets from American rivals as it grew into one of the world's biggest suppliers of wireless network equipment.

The case has shadowed Huawei since 2018. The following year, the US government placed the company on a trade blacklist restricting its access to American technology, and prosecutors announced the charges in 2019. The verdict closes the trial phase of an eight-year dispute, but the split decision leaves both sides with something to claim.

What happened

The Brooklyn trial ran for more than two months. Prosecutors called more than 60 witnesses; the defence called 15. Huawei and three affiliates faced 12 charges, including racketeering conspiracy, bank fraud, wire fraud, sanctions violations, and conspiring to steal trade secrets and obstruct justice.

The charges fell into two strands, the first concerning Iran. The 2018 indictment alleged that Huawei misled HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas about its business dealings in Iran, so it could move dollars in and out of the country in violation of US sanctions. "After the media started questioning Huawei's operations in Iran, so did Huawei's banks," prosecutor Meredith Arfa told jurors during her closing argument.

The Iran strand is also where the case began in public. In December 2018, Meng Wanzhou, Huawei's chief financial officer and the founder's daughter, was detained in Canada on a US warrant, prompting a diplomatic crisis involving the United States, China and Canada. Secretly indicted for allegedly misleading an HSBC banker about Huawei's business in Iran, she fought extradition for nearly three years, then entered a deferred prosecution agreement and flew home to China; the charges against her were dropped. Prosecutors gave jurors a statement attached to that agreement, in which she acknowledged the company illegally conducted business in Iran. She did not testify at the trial, though the HSBC banker she met in 2013 did.

The second strand concerned trade secrets. Charges added in 2020 alleged Huawei conspired to steal trade secrets from US technology companies over two decades. Prosecutors tried to prove Huawei stole Cisco Systems router source code, stole the antenna technology of wireless firm Quintel, and hired a professor to gain access to a circuit board developed by semiconductor company CNEX.

Some of the evidence was visual. Jurors watched a video that, prosecutors said, showed a Huawei employee slipping a piece of a T-Mobile testing robot into his backpack, and heard of a Huawei engineer sneaking into a Fujitsu booth at a trade show to photograph a device. Reuters stories from 2012 and 2013 helped expose Huawei's activities in Iran and were discussed during the trial.

A deeper look: eight years, two stories

The two sides told the jury very different stories. For the prosecution, the case was about a pattern: assistant US attorney Meredith Arfa said in closing arguments that Huawei spent 20 years doing whatever it took to expand, including lying to banks about Iran and stealing technology secrets from rivals for a competitive edge.

The defence told a story of competition and innovation instead. Huawei's lawyers argued that HSBC and the other banks knew about the company's business in Iran and were competing for its business. Brian Heberlig, a lawyer for Huawei, accused prosecutors of having "cherry-picked" a handful of disparate incidents to support their claim of a 20-year conspiracy, and said Huawei succeeded through "hard work, innovation, and investment in research and development."

The split verdict reflects both stories. The jury accepted the prosecution's case on the fraud and trade-secret counts — ten convictions — but rejected the claim Huawei operated as a criminal enterprise. Huawei seized on that, saying in a statement the racketeering acquittal "confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years."

The defence's argument about scale was a reminder of what is at stake beyond the courtroom. Huawei has more than 200,000 employees and operates in approximately 170 countries, and its lawyers presented its rise as the product of its own investment. Prosecutors accused Huawei of lying, cheating and stealing for a competitive edge; the defence said prosecutors had decided it was a criminal organisation and built their case from there.

Why it matters

For the Justice Department, the verdict is a victory in one of its highest-profile cases against a Chinese company, pursued for years on the grounds that Huawei posed national security risks. Convictions on bank fraud and trade-secret conspiracy give prosecutors a precedent for future cases about technology theft and sanctions evasion. The trial tested whether a jury would accept a sweeping, decades-long conspiracy theory. It did not, at least not in full.

For Huawei, the damage is real but bounded. It was convicted on most counts, and the verdict confirms in a US courtroom the allegations about its conduct in Iran and its treatment of rivals' technology. But the racketeering acquittal — the charge alleging it acted as a criminal enterprise — gives it a genuine talking point, and it has already used it.

For the wider technology industry, the most striking fact may be how little the case has slowed Huawei down. Experts have said it has not undermined the company's business: Huawei diversified and became a key player in China's push for artificial intelligence, despite being cut off from much American technology since 2019. A courtroom verdict is only one front in a contest running through supply chains, chip design and artificial intelligence — and Huawei is still very much in the race.

The verdict also lands as the United States and China spar over technology, trade and national security. The trial exposed, in open court, the methods prosecutors say Huawei used to grow — and the defences a company of its size can mount. For prosecutors, ten convictions out of twelve is a win. For Huawei, the one acquittal that mattered most is the one it will keep repeating.
