Vanity Fair's Taylor Swift Exposé: 'A Child Pretending to Be an Adult'

Taylor Swift has spent two decades as pop music's most carefully managed success story: the small-town songwriter who became a billionaire, a champion of artists' rights, and the star of the highest-grossing tour in history. On Monday, Vanity Fair published a profile that tells a very different story.

The lengthy piece, written by May Jeong and built on interviews with more than 100 sources, describes Swift as controlling and "punitive," and questions nearly every part of the image she has built for herself. Strikingly, it never uses her name at all. She is only "The Star," and almost everyone else in her orbit gets a nickname, too.

What happened

The profile is structured like a record. Its "A side" sections tell the familiar version of Swift's story — the outsider who fought her way to the top — while its "B side" sections offer a much less flattering account of the same life. It is that second version that has set the internet alight.

Many of the harshest claims come from anonymous sources. A music industry executive, referred to in the piece only as "The Exec," described Swift bluntly: "She is punitive." The same executive compared Swift's social circle to the villain of Mean Girls, saying, "It's truly Regina George."

Another source, described as a person who used to be close with Swift, said she "loves to smack people in the head" — figuratively, as a way of disciplining people — and has a pattern of cutting people out of her life. "Nobody is beyond reproach with her. If you don't see it her way, she comes after you," the source said.

The piece also runs through Swift's long list of feuds, including her rift with Katy Perry, who appears as "The California Girl." Jeong described Perry as "an adult pretending to be a child," then turned the line on Swift, writing that she "might be a child pretending to be an adult." According to Jeong, many people refused to speak on the record, and one former collaborator even advised her to turn down the assignment. Swift herself declined to comment.

A deeper look: the wedding, 'beyond want, greed'

The profile saves some of its sharpest writing for Swift's wedding to Kansas City Chiefs tight end Travis Kelce, held this summer at Madison Square Garden. Their romance began in 2023, after Kelce revealed he had tried to hand Swift a friendship bracelet bearing his phone number at one of her concerts. She began appearing at Chiefs games that fall, and the couple celebrated a Super Bowl win together in February 2024. In August 2025, Swift appeared on Kelce's New Heights podcast to announce her album The Life of a Showgirl; the couple got engaged later that month.

Jeong pokes fun at how publicly the romance unfolded — the games, the podcast, the Instagram posts — and at the idea that the two have much in common. "He practices; she rehearses. He plays games, she plays shows," she writes. Kelce, like Swift, is never named; Jeong writes that she must "regretfully" call him "Redwood."

The harshest criticism is reserved for the wedding itself, which took place in midtown Manhattan during a heat wave. Jeong writes that guests ate buffet food in the massive venue while thousands of New Yorkers lost power, and she takes aim at the couple's $26 million donation to local charities — noting that, relative to Swift's net worth, it was the equivalent of a New Yorker on the city's median household income giving away about $1,000.

The piece also details the reported extravagance, including a raffle in which guests reportedly won a Chanel bag and a 1970 Chevrolet Chevelle. Madison Square Garden reportedly locked out its own security staff, and even NYPD officers on site were not allowed to have their phones. "Love may be free, but the performance of it is not," Jeong wrote. The roughly 1,000 guests reportedly included at least one government contractor whose company took over the country's largest ICE detention facility in March.

Why it matters

The profile lands at a moment when the story Swift tells about herself is already under strain. For years, she has cast herself as an outsider abandoned by friends as a kid, turning her breakups, feuds and business battles into songs with her as the protagonist — often as the one who was wronged. The biggest of those battles came in 2019, when music executive Scooter Braun acquired her former label, Big Machine, and with it the masters to her first six albums. Swift answered with a scathing post on Tumblr, accusing Braun of "incessant, manipulative bullying," then re-recorded those albums as "Taylor's Version." In 2025, she bought back the original recordings, a moment widely celebrated as a win for artists' rights.

Her feuds have been just as public: with Kim Kardashian and Kanye West, with Katy Perry, and more recently with Charli XCX, whose 2024 song "Sympathy is a knife" was widely believed to be about Swift and whose "Actually Romantic" was seen as a pointed reply. Earlier this month, Swift appeared to end feud rumors with former friend Karlie Kloss at a Kansas City Chiefs game. Through it all, her popularity only grew: her Eras Tour became the highest-grossing tour of all time, and she became a billionaire in 2023.

But the last few weeks have been rocky. Swift was reportedly urged to "take back control" after details from the wedding leaked, she is caught in a legal battle over The Life of a Showgirl, and even one self-described superfan recently declared that "the blind love era is over." The Vanity Fair piece does not create that turbulence, but it gives it a shape — and a vocabulary — that will be hard to ignore.

It is worth remembering what the profile is, and what it is not. Its harshest claims rest on anonymous sources, many people refused to speak on the record, and Swift declined to comment. What is not in dispute is the reaction: within hours, the piece had become the story of the day, and the argument over who Taylor Swift really is — the underdog, or "The Star" — is now being held in public, on terms Vanity Fair chose.
