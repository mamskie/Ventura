Twin Waterspouts and a Tornado Warning Sweep the San Francisco Bay Area

Monday afternoon delivered the kind of weather the San Francisco Bay Area almost never sees. Twin waterspouts spun up over the bay near Candlestick Point, a tornado warning went out for the Santa Cruz coast, and hail rattled the East Bay — all on the same stormy day, with a stronger system lined up behind it.

The waterspouts formed east of Brisbane and north of San Francisco International Airport, two tall columns of water rising toward a low, gray sky and lasting several minutes. Video of the pair spread quickly online, and the National Weather Service confirmed the sightings — while warning that the same rotating air could turn dangerous ashore.

Nobody was hurt, and no damage was reported from the waterspouts. But the day showed how fast Bay Area weather can turn, and forecasters say the region is only partway through a wet week, with heavier rain, possible flooding and up to four feet of Sierra snow to come.

What happened on Monday

The warnings started early in the afternoon. At 1 p.m., the National Weather Service issued two tornado warnings for Santa Cruz County — one for the Boulder Creek area and one for Davenport — after tracking a severe thunderstorm about 10 miles west of Santa Cruz capable of producing a waterspout. The warning for a rural part of the county ran until 1:30 p.m., and no tornado was reported in either warned area.

In Davenport, the threat was close enough to send a school into shelter. Staff at Pacific Elementary School moved students indoors and away from windows. "We got everyone indoors, closed the doors and windows, then closed the blinds and curtains and then moved everyone away from the windows… and spent the rest of the time hunkered down," Eric Gross of the school said. Joel Kauffman, a Davenport parent, described the dark formation rolling in from the horizon: "Basically from almost sea level going up thousands of feet coming at you, like darkness that you can't see past. I couldn't tell because it was the horizon to horizon. It was a huge wall, stretching out to the ocean and up to the mountains."

Out on the bay, the spectacle was unfolding at the same time. A little after noon, the Weather Service issued a special marine warning for the waters of San Francisco Bay south of the Bay Bridge, and video captured two columns of water rising near Candlestick Point State Recreation Area. Colin McCarthy, founder of U.S. Stormwatch, filmed the pair, and National Weather Service meteorologist Roger Gass confirmed the sightings to SFGATE. Hail was reported inland in Richmond, rounding out a day of nearly every kind of severe weather.

The storm itself was rated a strong Level 2, and ABC7 meteorologist Drew Tuma said it could be bumped up to a Level 3 as it neared the region — a measure of a system already producing tornado warnings, waterspouts and hail before its heaviest rain.

When a waterspout becomes a tornado

A waterspout is a rotating column of air over water, and Monday's pair was not especially rare, in the Weather Service's assessment. What made them worth a warning is what happens if one comes ashore: a waterspout that moves inland and keeps its rotation becomes a tornado, and that is when the National Weather Service issues a tornado warning, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

"The conditions in the atmosphere are favorable for rotation and so we're seeing that being realized," Gass said. Waterspouts are also hard for forecasters to see coming: they are difficult to pick up on radar, Gass noted, which is why public sightings and shoreline video matter. The special marine warning for the bay is the marine equivalent of a severe thunderstorm warning, for coastal waters expecting winds of 34 knots or more — and for waterspouts.

For people on the water or near the shore, the advice is the same as for a tornado. "If a waterspout moves inland and contains its rotation, it becomes a tornado," Gass said. "So move into the interior portion of your home, stay away from windows, and put as many walls between you and the location you're in."

The sight was also a first in a long while for this stretch of water. NOAA's storm events database has no recorded waterspouts over San Francisco Bay in the last 28 years — a reminder that even a familiar phenomenon can startle when it shows up where it has not been seen in a generation.

Why it matters

The search surge is easy to explain: dramatic video of two waterspouts over one of the country's most photographed bays, plus a tornado warning where tornadoes are almost never forecast. But the story does not end with Monday's weather. A series of storms is lined up for the Bay Area over the next week, bringing heavy rain, flash flooding, gusty winds and possible debris flows — the next system arrives Tuesday night and runs into Friday morning, with the greatest impacts on Wednesday and Thursday.

The risks go beyond rain. Moderate to heavy rainfall could cause minor flooding, and areas scarred by recent wildfires face a higher risk of flash flooding, mud and debris flows; forecasters warned that some roads could become impassable. In the Sierra Nevada, a powerful winter storm is expected to dump up to four feet of snow this week, and the Weather Service warned mountain travel could become nearly impossible on Wednesday and Thursday. For Bay Area residents heading to Lake Tahoe, Tuesday daylight looks like the best window before conditions deteriorate — and Palisades Tahoe has already moved its opening day up to November 21.

Officials are asking residents to take the stretch seriously. Santa Clara County fire officials urged people to check road conditions before traveling and to prepare an emergency go-bag with medications, a first-aid kit, important documents and flashlights. Rain could linger through the weekend, so the wet pattern behind Monday's waterspouts is no one-day event.

Monday showed the warning system working as designed. The Weather Service tracked the storm, issued the warnings, and a school sheltered its students before anything touched down. No one was hurt and nothing was damaged — the best outcome for a day that gave the Bay Area a rare look at weather it usually watches from afar. The searches will fade; the storms, forecasters say, will linger longer.
