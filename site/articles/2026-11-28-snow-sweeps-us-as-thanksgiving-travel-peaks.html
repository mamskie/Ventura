Snow Sweeps the US as Thanksgiving Travel Peaks

Snow is the word of the Thanksgiving weekend in the United States. A powerful storm that swept the East Coast on the holiday knocked out power for more than 900,000 homes and flooded coastal towns — and as travellers head home, a second storm is bearing down on Colorado, where forecasters expect up to 15 inches of snow in the mountains on Saturday, with wind gusts of 50 to 60 mph.

The timing could hardly be worse. Saturday is the busiest return-travel day of the holiday weekend, and the Colorado storm will be at its worst on Saturday morning and into the early afternoon, exactly when mountain highways fill with cars heading home. Behind both storms sits a larger force: a very strong El Niño, shaping the storm track across the whole country.

What happened

The Thanksgiving storm tracked from the Southeast toward the Northeast, bringing heavy rain, strong winds and coastal flooding to much of the East Coast. Coastal flood warnings were posted from New Jersey to Virginia, and outages peaked at more than 900,000 on Thursday before falling to about 360,000 by Friday evening as crews restored power, according to poweroutage.us. Pennsylvania had the most outages, followed by Missouri.

The damage along the coast was vivid. In Virginia Beach, part of a fishing pier collapsed, closing a three-mile stretch of beach including the boardwalk. In Hampton, Virginia, the storm toppled a beloved landmark at Grandview Beach — a weathered, leafless tree known as the "Grieving Tree", whose branches visitors decorated with seashells. In Ocean City, New Jersey, floodwater kept Cheryl McKenna's family inside their home for six hours on the holiday, and in Avalon, merchants scrambled to pump out saltwater after some of the most significant tides in the community's history.

Snow reached well beyond the Northeast: part of Tennessee was under a winter storm warning for up to an inch of snow on Friday, after rain turned to light snow on Thanksgiving, and freezing rain moved across northern New England. In the Northwest, another system brought rain to coastal Oregon, with more snow forecast in the Cascades and in mountain passes in Montana and Wyoming.

Now Colorado is next. Winter storm warnings are in place across northwestern Colorado, including Rabbit Ears Pass, where 8 to 15 inches is expected, CBS News Colorado reported. Advisories extend south along the Interstate 70 mountain corridor, where 4 to 11 inches is forecast. Snow ramps up Friday night, with the heaviest falls on Saturday morning into the early afternoon. A strong cold front will drop across eastern Colorado on Saturday, bringing gusts of up to 60 mph to the southeastern plains; a high wind watch is in place from Colorado Springs and Pueblo east to the Kansas state line.

El Niño is setting the pattern

The storms are not arriving at random. A very strong El Niño — the warm phase of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation cycle — is under way, and forecasters say it is shaping the weather pattern across the country. El Niño occurs when surface waters in parts of the tropical Pacific become unusually warm, reaching at least 0.5 degrees Celsius above average, weakening or shifting the trade winds and altering the jet stream. The current event began earlier this year and is still strengthening, with parts of the Pacific more than 4 degrees Celsius above average, making it a "very strong" El Niño, according to scientists at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

"It certainly has an influence on what is happening this week," AccuWeather senior meteorologist Tom Kines told Newsweek. "Typically with El Niño, it gets stormy on the West Coast, and it certainly is. And it gets stormy across the south-central and Southeast states as well, and certainly that's happening." Kines added that storms linked to El Niño in the south can "creep farther north" into the Mid-Atlantic, Northeast, Ohio Valley and Great Lakes — which is what the Thanksgiving storm did.

The pattern has already left its mark: a relatively quiet Atlantic hurricane season, extreme rainfall that has pummelled parts of Florida in recent weeks, and a series of atmospheric rivers — narrow corridors of concentrated moisture — hitting the West Coast. NOAA's 2026-27 Winter Outlook favours wetter-than-average conditions from the Southwest through the Southeast and along the East Coast, while much of the north is expected to be warmer than average. A strong-to-very-strong El Niño is expected to persist through March of next year.

The snow map for the holiday stretched across the country. Illinois had the greatest potential, with 3 to 6 inches forecast around Springfield, according to AccuWeather, while 1 to 3 inches could reach Iowa near Davenport and Missouri around St. Louis. In the Pacific Northwest, the National Weather Service warned of up to 15 inches of snow in parts of Washington and Montana. On Black Friday, a rain-and-snow mix was possible across Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, New York, Pennsylvania, Maryland, West Virginia, Ohio, northern Kentucky, Indiana and Michigan.

Why it matters for the journey home

For millions of Americans, the question is not whether it will snow but when to travel. In Colorado, the advice is blunt: Saturday is the day to worry about. "If you're heading home for the holidays, Saturday is the day I'm most concerned about," CBS News Colorado meteorologist Joe Ruch said. His biggest concern is visibility: heavy snowfall rates combined with gusts of 50 to 60 mph could cut it to near zero in minutes, and fast-moving snow squalls — quick bursts of heavy, wind-driven snow — can turn good driving conditions dangerous without warning. In the southeastern plains, dry conditions mean the same winds will pick up dust.

Denver itself may see only a dusting, but even that would be historic: a tenth of an inch at Denver International Airport would end the city's wait for its first measurable snow of the season — and if it happens on Saturday, it would be the third-latest first snowfall on record. The consolation is that Sunday looks much better, with conditions expected to improve sharply once the storm clears.

The wider lesson is that a single climate pattern can shape a whole season of travel. El Niño winters can still produce significant snowstorms, even in regions that finish the season with below-average snowfall, and the jet stream's southward shift this winter favours a more active storm track across the southern United States. For anyone still on the road this weekend, the advice is the same from every forecaster: check conditions before leaving, allow extra time, and if the forecast looks bad, wait a day — the roads on Sunday will be a very different place from the roads on Saturday.
