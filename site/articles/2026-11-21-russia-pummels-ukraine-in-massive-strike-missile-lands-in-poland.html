Russia Pummels Ukraine in One of the War's Biggest Attacks — and a Missile Lands in Poland

Russia pounded Ukraine with one of the largest aerial assaults of the nearly five-year war overnight into Friday, firing scores of missiles and hundreds of drones, killing at least nine people and knocking out power across the country. The barrage came hours after President Vladimir Putin sat down with President Donald Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping for a trilateral summit in China billed as a step toward peace — and it ended with a missile landing inside Poland, a NATO member.

President Volodymyr Zelensky called the attack "the biggest this year." Ukrainian officials reported more than 80 missiles and some 400 drones fired in an assault lasting more than ten hours. In Poland, the government said a missile was confirmed to have landed in the eastern village of Nowy Lipowiec, with another missile and a jet-powered drone suspected at two other locations — an incursion Warsaw called a deliberate provocation.

What happened

Missiles came in waves, launched in groups separated by pauses of 20 to 30 minutes. Zelensky said the pauses were meant to slow firefighting, rubble-clearing and repairs. Of the 27 ballistic missiles Russia fired, Ukraine intercepted only three — a gap Kyiv blames on a critical shortage of missiles for its US-made Patriot air defense system.

In Kyiv, three bridges over the Dnipro were hit, Mayor Vitali Klitschko said, halting transport between the two sides of the capital. Seven people were killed in the capital, and two more died in Lviv, in the west of the country. AFP journalists saw residents sheltering in Kyiv's metro stations, sleeping on yoga mats and chairs. Kateryna Liubychenko, 32, took her toddler down to sleep through the bombardment. "It's painful to have to stop our child from living," she told AFP.

The strikes also tore into Ukraine's energy system as temperatures fall toward freezing. The state energy operator reported "significant damage" to infrastructure that had "seriously impacted the operation of the power grid." Odesa was in a "critical" situation with a widespread power outage, and blackouts were recorded in Kyiv and ten other regions. Emergency outages were imposed in three western regions, and the state-owned operator Ukrenergo called the grid situation "extremely critical."

Russia's military confirmed on Friday it had launched a "massive" attack, saying it targeted military-industrial sites, transport infrastructure, energy facilities and Odesa's port. Zelensky rejected that account. "Absolutely all the targets were ordinary civilian infrastructure — things that literally sustain people's lives," he said. Ukrainian officials said Moscow's targets included a hospital in Kharkiv and residential buildings in five regions.

Polish and NATO fighter jets were scrambled and Patriot air defense systems activated as objects crossed into Polish airspace. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the incursion was deliberate. "The provocation may have been linked with the fact that there are (military) exercises taking place on the territory of Poland," Tusk told a televised press conference in Warsaw, where thousands of European troops are drilling. "Nobody can say that this was an innocent coincidence," he added, saying he had informed NATO "straight away."

A deeper look: the summit that came just before the strikes

The assault came hours after Putin left China, following a day of meetings in Shenzhen with Trump and Xi. Putin called the summit historic, saying the talks took place in a "relaxed and very intimate atmosphere." But the talks produced no breakthrough, and Putin's declaration toward peace made no mention of Moscow's invasion.

Putin had arrived in China vowing a "painful" response to Ukraine's recent strikes on the strategic bridge linking Russia with occupied Crimea. "There will be no negotiations," he said. "I believe our response, as I have just said, will be far more painful than what the Kyiv regime did to the bridge."

After the summit, Trump spoke with European leaders and, according to a senior European official familiar with the call, described Putin as "tired" and ready for a deal, urging allies to "reach out and grasp at the opportunity." Tusk, who was on the call, said Trump sounded "fairly optimistic," but his words were received "very cautiously in the room." "I don't hide the fact that I've heard this very often from President Trump," Tusk said. "His belief in Putin's goodwill seems unshakable, even though so far there are no facts to support it."

Trump also called Putin a "friend" during the meeting — a remark that drew a sharp response from Zelensky. "If you say and speak about Putin ... like he is a friend ... he doesn't feel that he is a killer, he doesn't feel enough pressure," Zelensky told CNN. "For us, he is an enemy, and of course, for us he is a killer."

Why it matters

The attack matters first for what it did: it killed civilians, cut power as winter approaches and struck the bridges and grids that keep a capital moving. Russia has increased its long-range strikes as temperatures dip, and many in Ukraine fear this winter could be the worst of the war yet. The UN has reported rising civilian casualties this year, and UNICEF said on Friday that at least 114 children had been killed and 1,030 wounded in the first ten months of 2026 — a higher verified toll than in any of the past three years.

It matters second because of where a missile landed. Poland is a NATO ally, and an incursion into its territory — deliberate, in Warsaw's account, and timed to coincide with allied exercises — tests the alliance in a way a strike on Ukraine alone does not. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte called the incident "clearly dangerous" and condemned its spillover into allied territory; he is due in Warsaw on Saturday. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the EU stands in "full solidarity" with the Polish people, and France has requested a meeting of the UN Security Council.

And it matters because of the gap between the diplomacy and the bombing. A summit presented as a step toward peace was followed within hours by one of the war's biggest attacks, launched by a leader who had just said there would be no negotiations. That sequence will shape how European allies judge the next American message about Putin's intentions — whether they treat talk of a deal as hope or as a reason to demand proof. For Ukrainians sheltering in metro stations and waking to blackouts, the question is more immediate: how many more nights like this the winter will bring.
