DOJ Charges 800 in Largest Anti-Scam Crackdown Ever, Recovers $8 Billion

The United States Justice Department has announced the largest anti-scam enforcement surge in its history, charging more than 800 defendants and recovering over $8 billion in intended victim losses, officials said on Tuesday.

Attorney General Todd Blanche unveiled the results alongside FBI Director Kash Patel at a news conference in Washington, describing a months-long, government-wide campaign against the criminal networks behind the calls, texts and online schemes that now reach almost every American household. The announcement lands just days before Thanksgiving and the Black Friday shopping rush, when officials say fraudsters are at their busiest.

What happened

Federal prosecutors have filed more than 400 criminal cases across 85 federal districts since President Donald Trump signed an executive order in March directing a crackdown on fraud and predatory schemes targeting Americans. The cases have produced charges, extraditions, guilty pleas and prison sentences, according to the Justice Department.

The headline figures are striking. More than 800 defendants have been charged, and prosecutors say they have recovered over $8 billion in intended losses — money victims were on course to lose. Across the wider enforcement effort, federal agents and international partners have seized nearly $17 billion in illicit funds and confiscated more than 14,000 digital devices.

“We are not just going after individual scammers,” Blanche said. “We are dismantling the criminal networks that have been stealing from the American people for far too long.”

The operation, known as Operation Blackout, is an interagency campaign aimed at the international fraud syndicates behind the scams. Blanche said the effort would continue wherever the networks operate. “No matter where these criminals are in the world, we will find them and hold them accountable,” he said.

Inside the scam compounds

At the centre of the crackdown are the sprawling scam compounds that have sprung up overseas. According to Patel, federal agents and international partners have dismantled 29 of them across four continents, freeing more than 2,200 human trafficking victims forced to work inside them.

The compounds operate as self-contained towns, Patel said, where criminal networks force trafficked workers to run online fraud schemes against Americans around the clock. “These are literal towns where they force people into forced labor, traffic them, and then have them perpetrate this illicit activity against the American people,” he said.

The networks have proved mobile, shifting operations as law enforcement closes in. “They thought they could get away with it in Southeast Asia. They moved to Africa. They moved to the Middle East,” Patel said. “We annihilated them on every single continent that they chose to operate and attack American citizens.”

One enforcement push coordinated in London yielded 28 arrests across Africa, $11 million in seized assets, the rescue of 120 trafficked workers and the removal of 3.7 million fraudulent Facebook and Instagram accounts, Patel said.

Officials also highlighted individual cases that show how varied the schemes have become. In the past two weeks, federal authorities worked with Mexican officials to extradite a list broker accused of selling the names and contact information of more than one million elderly Americans to international scam networks, resulting in at least $15 million in victim losses. Agents dismantled websites impersonating United States federal courts and arrested nine suspects in an alleged sweepstakes scheme that defrauded seniors of millions of dollars.

In Georgia, authorities said drones were used to smuggle contraband mobile phones into prisons, where inmates allegedly used them to impersonate law enforcement officers and threaten Americans with arrest unless they paid fines for missing jury duty. One such scheme in Texas generated about $1 million in fraudulent proceeds and victimised hundreds of people across the country, according to US Attorney Ryan Raybould.

Why it matters

The crackdown comes as online and phone fraud continue to hit American households at staggering rates. Blanche cited a recent Gallup study finding that 10 percent of Americans reported that they or a household member fell victim to a scam in 2025, resulting in an estimated $68 billion in losses in a single year.

Beyond arrests and seizures, federal officials are also intervening before money leaves victims’ bank accounts. Through Operation Level Up, a joint effort with the US Secret Service, agents have directly warned more than 11,000 Americans that they were being targeted by scammers, preventing nearly $700 million in potential losses. Remarkably, 77 percent of those warned did not realise they were being scammed, Patel said.

The timing of the announcement is deliberate. With Black Friday and Cyber Monday approaching, officials urged shoppers to be sceptical of unsolicited calls, texts and delivery notifications, and to verify any urgent demand for payment through an independent channel. “Courts do not demand payment by cryptocurrency to avoid arrest. Law enforcement agencies do not direct citizens to Bitcoin kiosks,” Raybould said.

Blanche encouraged families to use Thanksgiving gatherings to talk about scams with parents, children and other relatives, and officials urged victims not to let embarrassment stop them reporting fraud. “You are not alone, and you should never be embarrassed,” Raybould said. “Your report may be the key that protects the next victim.” Americans can report suspected online fraud to the FBI at IC3.gov.

The message from the Justice Department was blunt: the scams persist because they work. “If the scammers stopped having success, they would stop doing it,” Blanche said. “So the reason why it’s worth it for them to send thousands of text messages is because it works.” For now, the department says, the surge will continue — and the networks behind the scams are running out of places to hide.
