Mamdani Sets 2032 Target to Close Rikers Island — Five Years Past the Legal Deadline

New York City’s Rikers Island jail complex will stay open until 2032 under a plan announced Friday by Mayor Zohran Mamdani — five years past the date the law requires it to close. The plan, the mayor’s first detailed blueprint for shuttering the notorious facility, hinges on cutting the number of people held there from more than 6,000 to around 4,500, then moving the rest into four smaller, more modern jails spread across the city’s boroughs.

For a jail that has stood as a symbol of dysfunction for decades, the announcement was both a milestone and a concession. The city is all but certain to miss the legal requirement to close Rikers and replace it with borough jails by 2027, and the new timeline admits what officials have known for months: the replacement jails are years behind schedule, and none has been built.

“It is time to finally close Rikers Island,” Mamdani said in a statement. “For decades, New Yorkers have demanded a correctional system that is safe and humane.”

What happened

Mamdani, a Democrat who has long campaigned to close Rikers, announced the plan on Friday. The strategy rests on shrinking the jail population first: from more than 6,500 people housed at Rikers, according to the Associated Press, to around 4,500.

The administration is proposing a slate of measures to get there — new diversion and supervised release programs, expanded housing and social services aimed at preventing repeat offending, restoring an online bail payment system, and cutting the court delays that keep people waiting behind bars. The city also plans to reduce the number of beds for women, diverting them into community-based programs instead.

Instead of housing people on Rikers — an island in the East River near LaGuardia Airport — the city will shift them to jails in the Bronx, Queens, Brooklyn and Manhattan, plus a handful of medical facilities for people who need health care or psychiatric treatment. City Hall says the borough jails are expected to open in Brooklyn in 2029, in the Bronx and Queens in 2031, and in Manhattan in 2032 — the same year Rikers is now supposed to close.

The plan arrives after months of quiet groundwork. The New York Post reported that the mayor’s office has been meeting with the city’s district attorneys since at least the summer, asking whether any cases in which people were ordered held on bail could be revisited to thin out the jail population. Officials in the Mayor’s Office of Criminal Justice met with representatives from the district attorneys’ offices as recently as Wednesday, discussing alternatives to jail such as men’s supportive housing and supervised release. Prosecutors were also asked to put together a “case study” of potential early-release candidates — but found that few suspects could actually be freed, and that reviewing each case would be slow, difficult work.

A deeper look: a deadline the city cannot meet

The 2032 target is an admission that the original plan has fallen apart. In 2019, the New York City Council voted to close Rikers and replace it with newer jails in the boroughs, setting a deadline of August 31, 2027. Lawmakers invoked the names of former prisoners who died there, including Kalief Browder, who took his own life at age 22 after being held at Rikers for three years.

More than half a decade and $16 billion later, none of the borough jails has been built, and the earliest the first one would open is 2029, according to the Post. The delay has put Mamdani in a difficult spot: his own allies on the left have pushed back against extending the deadline, with Councilmembers Sandy Nurse and Tiffany Caban objecting during a council briefing, while City Council Speaker Julie Menin said she was “committed to extending that deadline,” adding, “Right now, where we are is we cannot meet the council deadline.”

The population problem is the core of it. Around 3,300 people at Rikers — about 48 percent of the daily population — are facing violent charges, according to a 2026 City Hall report. That makes rapid depopulation hard: prosecutors told the mayor’s office that few bail cases could be revisited, and no direct request was made for cashless bail in upcoming cases.

Rikers’ reputation is what made closing it a political cause in the first place. The network of jails has been plagued for years by violence and overcrowding, and a federal judge, citing “abnormal and unacceptable” conditions, appointed an outside manager to restore order and carry out reforms. Criminal justice activists have pushed for decades to close a complex that has housed jail inmates since the 1930s. Mamdani’s predecessor, former Mayor Eric Adams, opposed federal oversight and the borough jails plan.

The new plan also includes a step first announced back in June: the demotion next summer of Rikers’ North Infirmary Command, a unit within the complex.

Why it matters

The 2032 date matters because it reframes a broken promise as a plan — and because it tests whether a mayor elected in part on closing Rikers can deliver it. The jail has become a measure of whether New York can run a correctional system that is, in Mamdani’s words, “safe and humane,” and the new timeline gives the city six more years to prove it.

It also matters for the people inside. More than 6,000 people are held at Rikers on any given day, and the conditions there — violence, overcrowding, federal oversight — are the reason the closure campaign exists. Every year the complex stays open is another year those conditions persist, and another year the borough jails that were supposed to replace it remain unbuilt.

The politics are as complicated as the logistics. Mamdani needs the City Council to pass legislation extending the deadline, and his own left flank is already objecting. At the same time, the administration’s quiet outreach to prosecutors shows it is trying to shrink the population now, before the new jails exist — a task prosecutors say is slow and limited.

For now, the search surge tells its own story: “rikers island” was among the most searched terms in the United States on Friday as the news broke, with people checking when the jail will close and what will replace it. The answer, for now, is 2032 — five years later than the law demands, and a date the city will have to defend, year by year, until it arrives.
