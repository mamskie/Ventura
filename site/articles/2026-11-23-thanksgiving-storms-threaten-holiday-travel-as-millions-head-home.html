Thanksgiving Storms Threaten Holiday Travel as Millions Head Home

Thanksgiving week is arriving with a warning for the millions of Americans heading home for the holiday: two coastal storms are lining up to hit the busiest travel days of the year. From Washington, D.C., to Boston, travelers have a short window of dry weather on Tuesday and much of Wednesday before a coastal storm moves in, bringing downpours and strong winds on Thanksgiving Day itself — weather that could reach all the way to the 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York City.

It is the second storm system to threaten the East Coast in a matter of days. Heavy rain last week triggered flooding and disrupted travel along Florida's Atlantic Coast, and forecasters say the new system will be followed by trouble on the other side of the country, too: a separate storm is heading for the San Francisco Bay Area, where unusually high tides could flood shoreline roads even before the rain arrives.

What happened

For anyone driving through the coastal Carolinas, Wednesday could be the toughest day on the road. A storm strengthening off the Southeast coast could bring downpours heavy enough to flood roads in parts of North and South Carolina, and as it moves north, rain could reach Washington, D.C., and Philadelphia by Wednesday night, setting up a wet drive along Interstate 95 on Thanksgiving morning. The Weather Prediction Center says 1 to 2 inches of rain could fall along the I-95 corridor, with up to 3 inches possible in localized areas, and how far inland the heaviest rain reaches will depend on how closely the storm hugs the coast.

On the West Coast, the trouble starts with the water, not the rain. While Monday looks mostly dry in San Francisco, the National Weather Service expects several road closures along the low-lying shores of San Francisco and San Pablo bays, with the highest water expected on Wednesday morning. Authorities are bracing for potential impacts to roads and infrastructure, including San Francisco International Airport, as the storm itself arrives on Tuesday.

In Boston, the roughest weather is expected on Thanksgiving Day and into the evening. Wind-driven rain could slow drivers, strong gusts could delay flights at Logan International Airport, and flooding is possible along the Massachusetts coast if the strongest onshore winds coincide with high tide. Travelers crossing the central Appalachians or driving through higher elevations in upstate New York and New England could encounter snow, although how much of it sticks is still uncertain. The drive home should turn drier around Washington, D.C., by Friday, even if it stays gusty in spots, while rain and snow showers could linger into Saturday in northern New England. Forecasters are also watching a separate storm that could form off the East Coast over the weekend, but it is too early to say whether it would affect Sunday travel.

A deeper look: the parade's 100th birthday, at the mercy of the wind

In New York City, it is the wind, not the rain, that could ground the celebration. The Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade is marking its 100th edition with a slew of new, high-flying balloons, but winds are expected to strengthen on Wednesday night, with widespread gusts of 35 to 45 mph forecast across the New York area on Thursday and higher gusts near the coast. City rules call for grounding the giant balloons if sustained winds exceed 23 mph or gusts top 34 mph, so the question is whether winds along the Manhattan parade route reach those speeds between the 8:30 a.m. start and the noon finish. With the forecast details still coming into focus, it is too early to tell.

While the weather dominates the travel conversation, the other half of the holiday is the meal itself — and this year the table is wider than ever. The Chicago Tribune's Thanksgiving guide notes that no day stresses vegans more, with hosts either cooking a fully vegan dinner or bringing a dish to a family table. Seasoned vegans, the guide says, tend to skip the main course altogether and build their meal from sides and savory dishes, with plant-based staples such as almond milk, nutritional yeast and chia seeds doing the work a turkey used to do. For millions of households, in other words, the planning this week is happening on two fronts at once: the road, and the kitchen.

Why it matters

Thanksgiving is the biggest travel week of the American year, and the timing of these storms could hardly be more pointed. They are set to hit each leg of the journey in turn: the Wednesday departures, the Thursday parade, and possibly the Sunday return, with a separate system already being watched off the East Coast for the weekend. A forecast that shifts by a few dozen miles changes which cities get soaked and which ones stay dry, so the picture will keep moving until the storms actually arrive.

The parade's centennial raises the stakes further. A 100th edition, new balloons in the sky, and a wind rule written in hard numbers — 23 mph sustained, 34 mph gusts — means the decision about the balloons will be made in miles per hour, not in sentiment. For the organizers, the marching bands and the crowds lining Sixth Avenue, the forecast is not background noise this year; it is the show.

For travelers, the practical message is simple: the window is Tuesday into Wednesday. After that, expect a wet Interstate 95, delays at Logan, and closures around the Bay Area, with Friday turning drier around Washington, D.C. The holiday itself will go ahead — the storms are setting the schedule, not cancelling the celebration.
