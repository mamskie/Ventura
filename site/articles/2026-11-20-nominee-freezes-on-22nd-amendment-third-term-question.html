A Judicial Nominee Froze on the 22nd Amendment — and the Third-Term Question Came Back With It

It was a simple question, the kind a nominee for a federal judgeship might expect to answer without thinking. During a Senate Judiciary Committee confirmation hearing on Wednesday, Senator Chris Coons of Delaware asked James P. Sullivan, a nominee for a federal district court seat, to describe what the 22nd Amendment provides. Sullivan paused. “Senator, if I’m remembering correctly — and I carry it over my heart because sometimes it isn’t in my brain — but if I, if I’m remembering it correctly, it is…” he said, and then trailed off. “Oh boy, oh boy.”

The exchange, which quickly spread online and sent searches for the 22nd Amendment surging across the United States, did not end there. When Coons pressed Sullivan on whether President Donald Trump — elected in 2016 and again in 2024 — could run for a third term in 2028, Sullivan declined to answer, citing judicial ethics rules and calling the question a matter of “ongoing political debate.” Coons called the answer “disappointing,” adding that the amendment’s meaning “strikes me as just bracingly clear on the face of it.”

What happened

The hearing, held on Wednesday, was a confirmation session for seven nominees, and Sullivan was one of them. He is a recent pick of Trump’s and a lame-duck justice on the Texas Supreme Court who lost his seat on Election Day to Charli Kelly, only to be nominated by the president weeks later to become a federal district judge. His nomination came as the administration pushed its picks through the Senate before a Democratic majority takes over the upper chamber.

The moment that drew attention came when Coons framed his question around the oath every federal judge takes. “Since we’re just talking about the importance of taking the oath to uphold and defend the Constitution,” Coons asked, “Mr. Sullivan, can you just tell me what the 22nd Amendment provides?” After Sullivan’s long pause and trailing answer, Coons stepped in to help him along. “No person shall be elected…” the senator offered, and Sullivan chimed in: “Ah yes! No person shall be elected more than twice, I believe, to the office of the president.”

Coons then moved to the question behind the question. “Has President Trump been elected and served, now twice, as president?” he asked. Sullivan began to answer — “President Trump has been certified as the winner in the 2016 and the 2024—” — before Coons cut him off and asked again, more directly: “Is he eligible to run for president again in 2028?”

Sullivan would not say. “So, Senator, as a nominee for a federal district court, it would be a violation I believe of Canon 5 for me to opine on something like that,” he replied. Canon 5 is a code-of-conduct rule stating that judges and judicial candidates should not take part in inappropriate political activity. “And so respectfully I’m not so sure that I can,” Sullivan continued. “I can certainly tell you how I’d think about approaching a problem like that, but this seems like both — it certainly seems like it’s the sort of thing that is a subject of ongoing political debate.”

A deeper look: what the 22nd Amendment actually says

The text at the centre of the exchange is short and direct. “No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice,” the 22nd Amendment begins. Ratified in 1951, after Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected to four terms, it turned the two-term tradition that had held since George Washington into a constitutional rule. This year marks its 75th anniversary.

Under that rule, the question Coons asked has a settled answer in the eyes of most constitutional scholars: a president elected twice cannot be elected a third time. Trump won the presidency in 2016 and won it again in 2024, which means the amendment bars him from being elected to a third term in 2028. That is the distinction at the heart of the discussion around his recent comments — expressing an interest in running is not the same as being eligible to win.

Trump himself has talked about the idea in both directions. In an interview with journalist Miranda Devine on her Pod Force One podcast, he said he would “love to run” in the 2028 election, while also calling the constitutional language “very exacting.” Devine discussed the interview on Fox Business on 1 November, saying Trump had “come to terms with the fact that he won’t be running again.” In an earlier exchange with reporters, Trump was more direct still: “I would say that, if you read [the Constitution], it’s pretty clear. I’m not allowed to run. It’s too bad.” His remarks expressed enthusiasm for another campaign without announcing one or suggesting a way around the restriction.

Even so, the third-term question has refused to go away. Since returning to office, Trump has floated using “methods” to run for a third term and told reporters he would “love” to stay past 2028. That is why a nominee’s stumble over the amendment’s text, in a hearing about fitness for the federal bench, landed as more than a trivia slip.

Why it matters

For a judicial nominee, the stakes of a moment like this are straightforward. Federal judges spend their careers interpreting the Constitution, and senators use confirmation hearings to test whether a nominee knows it, respects it and will say plainly what it means. A nominee who cannot describe one of its best-known amendments — and then will not say whether its limit applies to the president who nominated him — gives senators reason to ask what else might go unanswered once he is on the bench.

The exchange also matters because of the moment it arrived in. Talk of a third term has persisted even though the constitutional text is plain, and every public figure who treats the question as open helps keep it open. When a nominee for a lifetime judgeship declines to call the matter settled, it lends weight to a debate the amendment was written to end — a rule “bracingly clear on the face of it,” met with an answer that was anything but.

What happens next is procedural: Sullivan’s nomination will move through the committee and, if it advances, to a vote of the full Senate, where the hearing exchange is now part of his record. The broader question — whether talk of a third term fades or builds toward 2028 — will be answered by the president’s own words and by the voters and courts who hear them. For now, the episode has sent Americans back to the text of the 22nd Amendment itself. The answer, written in 1951, has not changed.
