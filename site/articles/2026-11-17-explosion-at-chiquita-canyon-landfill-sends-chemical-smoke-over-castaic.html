Explosion at Chiquita Canyon Landfill Sends Chemical Smoke Over Castaic

A reported explosion during work at the Chiquita Canyon Landfill sent a thick column of black smoke into the sky over Castaic on Monday afternoon, drawing firefighters and an evacuation advisory for nearby neighborhoods — and pushing a troubled landfill back into the national spotlight.

The incident was first reported just after 4 p.m., and it arrived at a site that has been at the center of a public health and environmental crisis for years. The 639-acre landfill off Highway 126 stopped accepting waste at the start of 2025, but an underground chemical reaction smoldering beneath it since 2022 has kept the facility — and the communities around it — in the news ever since.

What exactly escaped into the air on Monday is still being determined. What is known so far comes from fire officials, radio dispatch traffic and local reporting: an explosion during drilling work, chemicals released into the air, several workers evaluated for exposure, and an advisory telling nearby residents to be ready to leave. Here is what the reporting supports — and what it does not yet answer.

What happened on Monday afternoon

Los Angeles County firefighters were called to the landfill on Monday afternoon after a plume of thick black smoke was seen rising from the site just after 4 p.m., according to The Signal, the Santa Clarita Valley's local newspaper. The smoke was visible across the area, and the county sheriff's Santa Clarita Valley station soon posted a warning on social media: "Avoid the Area: L.A. County Fire is responding to an incident near Henry Mayo Drive and Highway 126. Thick black smoke is visible in the area."

Radio dispatch traffic from firefighters on the scene, as reported by The Signal, indicated that landfill personnel told them an explosion had occurred during work at the landfill and that "chemicals" were escaping into the air. According to that dispatch traffic, there was no fire burning after the explosion. ABC7 Los Angeles reported that workers were apparently drilling a pipe about 150 feet deep when the release occurred, and described the event as a large chemical release.

Landfill personnel were removed from the facility while firefighters evaluated the scene. Several people were checked by first responders to see whether they would need decontamination, and as of about 5 p.m. it was expected that around half a dozen of them would be taken to Henry Mayo Newhall Hospital for monitoring, though none were reported to have serious injuries. ABC7 reported that at least three people were being evaluated at the scene.

As the response unfolded, the sheriff's station issued an evacuation advisory for five zones — CAS-VALVERDE-A, CAS-HASLEY, CAS-HILLCREST, CAS-CAMBRIDGE and CAS-INDUSTRY — telling residents in those areas to be prepared to evacuate if instructed by authorities. The advisory was a warning, not an evacuation order. Shortly after 5 p.m., firefighters on the scene reported that the plume had "significantly diminished."

A landfill with a long, troubled history

Monday's incident did not happen at an ordinary landfill. The Castaic facility, just south of Val Verde, has been at the center of a public health and environmental crisis since an underground chemical reaction known as an elevated temperature landfill event began in early 2022. The reaction has produced heat, odors and gases deep inside the waste mass, and it has not stopped simply because the gates closed to new trash on January 1, 2025.

Federal regulators had already found the site in violation of clean-air rules. In June 2024, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency notified the landfill's operators that they were in violation of the federal Clean Air Act, citing excessive toxic air emissions in a letter dated June 4. The notice went to the operators, Chiquita Canyon LLC, and to the company that owns the landfill, Texas-based Waste Connections. According to the EPA, the landfill was emitting high levels of toxic gases such as benzene and volatile organic compounds, which are known to create smog and contribute to the formation of ozone, which can cause lung damage.

The elevated temperature event, its effects on nearby residents and the way it has been handled by Waste Connections are now the focus of multiple lawsuits, including one filed last month by the state of California. That legal pressure, and years of complaints from people living near the site, are part of why a single afternoon of black smoke drew such a fast and visible response — and why the story spread so quickly beyond the Santa Clarita Valley.

The landfill's history also explains the caution in the official response. Workers were checked for contamination before anyone declared the scene safe, an evacuation advisory went out while the plume was still rising, and hospitals were put on standby for monitoring rather than treatment. Officials were treating the release as a chemical event, not a routine fire, and that distinction shaped every decision in the first hour.

Why it matters

The search surge around Chiquita Canyon Landfill is easy to understand: a visible column of black smoke, an explosion reported during work, and an evacuation advisory in a populated area will always draw attention. But the reason the story matters goes beyond a single afternoon. A landfill that stopped taking waste nearly two years ago can still produce a chemical emergency, because the reaction burning underground did not end when the trucks stopped coming.

For the communities around Castaic and Val Verde, Monday was a reminder that the crisis at Chiquita Canyon is not history. Residents have lived for years with a site under federal violation notices, under lawsuits and under an underground reaction that officials have struggled to bring under control. Each new incident tests the emergency plans built around the site — and each one asks the same question: how much risk remains buried there?

Much is still unknown. Authorities have not yet said which chemicals escaped, in what amounts, or what caused the explosion during the drilling work. ABC7 described its report as a developing story that would continue to be updated, and the early accounts rest on dispatch traffic and first reports rather than a completed investigation. Until officials release test results and a fuller account of what happened, the confirmed facts are the smoke, the response, the advisory and the injuries that were not serious — and the longer history that made all of it feel familiar to the people who live nearby.
