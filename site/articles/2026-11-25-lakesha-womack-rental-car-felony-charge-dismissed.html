LaKesha Womack Cleared as Rental Car Felony Charge Is Dismissed After Election Win

A felony charge that shadowed LaKesha Womack through the final weeks of her campaign is gone. On Tuesday, prosecutors in Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, voluntarily dismissed the charge of failing to return a rented motor vehicle against the congresswoman-elect, closing a case that began with a 2019 Toyota Corolla rental and resurfaced, unannounced, at a campaign rally six weeks before Election Day.

The dismissal, announced by District Attorney Spencer Merriweather, removes the last legal obstacle before Womack takes her seat in Congress. She is scheduled to be sworn in on Sunday, January 3, 2027, at the United States Capitol, after defeating incumbent Republican Representative Tim Moore in one of the most surprising upsets of the 2026 midterms.

Womack, a 44-year-old Democrat, beat Moore by 2.2 percentage points — 147,920 votes to 141,353 — in North Carolina’s 14th Congressional District. The charge had become a campaign issue in the race’s final stretch, seized on by Republicans and covered intensely in the Charlotte media. That she won anyway, and that the case has now been dropped, turns a story about a legal cloud into one about a remarkably short-lived political attack.

What happened

The case dates back to August 27, 2019, when Womack rented a 2019 Toyota Corolla. She extended the rental through October 7, 2019, but the car was not returned. Law enforcement recovered it on December 4, 2019 — undamaged — and prosecutors charged her with failure to return a rented motor vehicle on January 14, 2020, forty-one days after the car had already been handed back.

Then the case went quiet. There was no activity from February 2020 until October 11, 2026, when the criminal summons was reissued and served on Womack at a campaign rally — just weeks before the November 3 election. The timing turned a dormant, six-year-old case into a live campaign controversy overnight.

After the election, the case moved quickly toward resolution. An initial court date was set for November 10, and Womack’s attorney appeared on her behalf. On November 16, the district attorney’s office was informed that Womack and the rental company had reached an agreement: she paid the company an assessed rental cost covering the period from the day the car was deemed overdue until it was returned. Representatives of the company then told prosecutors they no longer wanted to pursue the case.

On Tuesday, November 24, Merriweather’s office announced it would voluntarily dismiss the charge, and online court records showed the dismissal had taken place. The dismissal was with prejudice, meaning the charge cannot be refiled.

A deeper look: six years on ice, then a rally

The most striking feature of the case is its timeline. A charge filed in January 2020 sat unserved for more than six years, then was reissued on a single October day in the middle of a congressional campaign — and served in the most public way possible, at a rally, in front of supporters and cameras.

That sequence is what made the story national news. For Womack’s opponents, the summons was a ready-made attack: a Democrat running for Congress while facing a felony charge. For her supporters, the timing looked like something else — a dormant case revived at the moment it could do the most political damage. The district attorney’s office has not suggested the timing was political; the record shows only that a summons issued in 2020 went unserved and was reissued in October 2026.

The substance of the case, meanwhile, was narrow. The car was returned undamaged, and the dispute was over money — the rental cost for the weeks the car was kept beyond its return date. Once Womack paid that assessed cost, the company stepped aside, and the prosecution had no complaining witness left. Cases built on a victim’s complaint often end that way: when the victim is made whole and walks away, the state’s interest in prosecuting fades with it.

The outcome also fits the charge’s history. A summons that goes unserved for six years is not a case prosecutors were pressing; it is a file that sat still until a routine reissuance put it back in motion. The case did not collapse because of a dramatic courtroom fight. It ended the way it began — quietly, with paperwork — once the bill at its centre was paid.

Why it matters

For Womack, the dismissal clears the way to Congress without a pending criminal case hanging over her first days in office. She will be sworn in on January 3, 2027, representing a district that spans part of Mecklenburg and Polk counties and all of Burke, Cleveland, Gaston and Rutherford counties, including Charlotte’s western suburbs, Gastonia, Shelby and Morganton.

For the district, the result stands as one of the cycle’s biggest surprises. Moore was North Carolina’s former House speaker, seeking his second term in Congress. Womack beat him by 2.2 points in a race few forecasters had on their upset lists — and she did it while a felony summons was fresh news.

For voters, the episode is a reminder of how quickly an old file can become a campaign weapon — and how quickly it can dissolve once the underlying dispute is settled. The charge dominated coverage in the campaign’s final weeks; within three weeks of the election, it was gone. What remains is the result: a new congresswoman, a closed case, and a district that changed hands.
