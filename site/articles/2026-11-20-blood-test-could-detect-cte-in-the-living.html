A Blood Test That Could Detect CTE in the Living Is Closer Than Ever

For decades, the diagnosis came too late. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, the degenerative brain disease linked to repeated head impacts, could only be confirmed by examining a person’s brain after death. That meant the athletes, soldiers and others living with its symptoms — mood swings, memory problems and changes in behaviour — could never know for certain what was happening inside their own heads. A study published on Thursday in the Journal of the American Medical Association suggests that may be about to change.

Researchers at Boston University and Banner Health in Arizona report that a blood test can identify the most severe cases of CTE in living people. The test looks for a telltale protein fragment in the blood, and in the study it cleanly separated former football players with advanced CTE from those with mild or no disease. The work is early — the sample was small, and the test is nowhere near a doctor’s office — but outside experts are calling it a turning point.

What happened

The study, led by scientists at Boston University’s CTE Center, drew on 21 former football players who had given blood while they were alive and later donated their brains to the university’s brain bank after they died. When researchers examined those brains, 18 of the 21 players had CTE, including 16 with high-stage, or advanced, disease.

In the blood samples, the team measured levels of a biomarker called endogenously cleaved microtubule-binding region tau — eMTBR-tau for short. Players with high-stage CTE carried far higher levels of it than players with low-stage CTE or none at all. “This is a game changer,” Michael Alosco, the study’s senior author and co-director of clinical research at the BU CTE Center, said in a statement. “If future studies validate these findings, this blood test will allow us to detect CTE with a high level of accuracy and open the doors to clinical trials and treatment.”

The study is the largest yet in which blood was collected from people while they were alive and then matched to a post-mortem diagnosis, according to Annalise Miner, a Boston University researcher and one of the study’s authors. She called the findings “a great first step,” while cautioning that the work still needs to be repeated in a larger group of people.

It was not the only result of its kind this week. Days earlier, a second paper, also published in JAMA, reported that the same biomarker — measured with a different laboratory method, mass spectrometry — was elevated in people with severe cases of tau-related diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease and CTE. Together, the two papers make the case that a blood signal for CTE is real, and that more than one laboratory can find it.

There are limits, and the researchers are open about them. The test is not approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the sample was small, and the study focused on former football players — a narrow group. “We still have more work to do before you can bring it back to patients,” Alosco said. “But it’s an exciting first step.”

A deeper look: how a blood test could spot a brain disease

To understand the test, it helps to understand what CTE does to the brain. Like Alzheimer’s disease, CTE is marked by an abnormality in a protein called tau. In a healthy brain, tau helps hold the structure of brain cells together. In CTE, tau misfolds, tangling and clumping inside brain cells and slowly damaging them. The biomarker at the centre of the new study, eMTBR-tau, is a fragment of that tangled tau that escapes into the bloodstream — a faint echo of the damage, detectable with a blood draw.

There is a complication. The same biomarker is also elevated in people with Alzheimer’s disease, so a high reading on its own cannot say which disease a person has. That is where a second biomarker comes in. A protein fragment called p-tau217 is known to track closely with Alzheimer’s, but not with CTE. In theory, a two-part blood test — one marker that catches both diseases, one that catches only Alzheimer’s — could tell them apart. Alosco says further testing is needed to “detect and differentiate” between the two conditions.

Outside experts were struck by how quickly the field has moved. “It is a watershed moment for the field, demonstrating that fluid biomarkers can work in assisting diagnosis of CTE in the living,” said Michael Buckland, a clinical associate professor of pathology at the University of Sydney in Australia, who was not involved in either new study. “A confident CTE diagnosis in the living opens the door for clinical trials to start finding treatments for the disease.”

The reason a test matters so much is practical. Without a way to measure CTE in a living person, researchers cannot run the kind of clinical trial that proves whether a treatment works. “You can’t have something like a clinical trial without being able to measure a pathology during life,” Miner said. “So with CTE, we can’t currently measure it. So having that biomarker will help the acceleration of clinical trials and hopefully treatments in the future.”

Why it matters

For the families and former players who have lived with the uncertainty, the stakes are immediate. CTE has been linked to depression, aggression, impulsivity and memory loss, and its shadow has hung over American football for more than a decade. A test that could identify the disease early would give people answers — and, just as importantly, a way into trials of treatments that might slow it down.

For science, a test would answer a question nobody has been able to settle: how common is CTE, really? Because the disease can only be diagnosed after death, and because brains are usually donated by families who already suspect something was wrong, nobody knows how many people carry it. A blood test would let researchers screen far more widely — among athletes, military veterans and others exposed to repeated head impacts — and find out.

That is still a way off. The next step is to test the biomarker in a much larger group of people, and to confirm that it works beyond former football players. But for a field that has spent years studying a disease it could only see after death, the direction of travel has changed. For the first time, doctors can imagine a future in which CTE is found in a living patient, in time to do something about it.
